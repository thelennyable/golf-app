## The one sentence (what the whole book is about)

The town is still here and a surprising amount of it is still standing, but the life that filled it was taken apart a piece at a time, and what people are really mourning is not the buildings but the kid they were inside them.

(Evidence it runs through the whole book, not one chapter: the old school, the Strand, the Youth Center, Jim's, the McCray house, the Klinkenberg and Palace buildings and the Harman's building are all still standing, and in chapter after chapter the loss is named as the person, not the place: "It is the kid waiting on it on a Friday night who is gone," "It is only the kids who are not kids anymore," "The Saturday morning kid you were is still in there somewhere," "You just cannot walk back out at fourteen.")

## Title candidates

The title is Lenny's decision. Three candidates, one of each shape plus a second Shape B.

**1. Kendallville, Indiana: When We Walked Everywhere** (Shape B, no subtitle)
- Where it came from: composed, not lifted. It names a finding that runs through nearly every chapter: you walked uptown, walked to the corner store, walked to school down the alley, walked to the Palace and to Puritan's, walked home from practice in the dark, watched your father walk home from the foundry across the field, walked home down Dowling after the tornado.
- Risk: plain rather than lyrical; a buyer may read it as a book about walking or about the town before cars, and the bikes (which are everywhere in the chapters too) are left out.
- Cold test: passes. Anyone who grew up in a small town before the 1990s owns the phrase; it needs no chapter.
- Proper-noun test: only the town's own name, which is always allowed.
- Subtitle coherence: no subtitle needed. The phrase agrees with the thesis: the town is still there, the walking life is what went.

**2. One More Walk Through Town / Growing Up in Kendallville, Indiana** (Shape A)
- Where it came from: composed from the town's recurring wish rather than lifted from a line. The old-school chapter records the wish "to walk those halls one more time," and Walking Uptown is the Saturday walk store to store; the phrase widens that wish to the whole town.
- Risk: the searchable word, Kendallville, sits in the subtitle field, which carries less weight and gets truncated; and it is a near sibling of Jim Smith's *Walk Through Town* (a boyhood memoir of Tuscumbia, Alabama).
- Cold test: passes. The wish to walk your hometown one more time as it was needs no explanation.
- Proper-noun test: no landmark named.
- Subtitle coherence: "Growing Up in Kendallville, Indiana" agrees with the thesis and does not imply the town is gone. The series formula "A Portrait of the Kendallville That Was" was considered and rejected, because it argues with the one sentence above: the town is still standing.

**3. Kendallville, Indiana: Loved That Place** (Shape B, no subtitle)
- Where it came from: the town's own words, recurring across several chapters in slightly different forms ("Loved that place" for the Palace and Puritan's, "Loved that store" for Harman's, "Loved Bud and Maxine," "I loved North Side").
- Risk: on the cover "that place" reads as Kendallville itself, in the past tense, which hints the town is gone or no longer loved: the same misreading that cost Fairfield its subtitle, and it argues with the thesis.
- Cold test: passes as a feeling, but the clipped quote-voice can read like a review snippet.
- Proper-noun test: only the town's own name.

**Which I would ship: #1, Kendallville, Indiana: When We Walked Everywhere.** It puts the searchable word first, it is true of the whole book rather than one chapter, and it is the only one of the three with no argument against the thesis.

Rejected before shortlisting: *Still in There Somewhere* (lifted from the last line of the bowling chapter; fails the cold test, the reader cannot tell what is in where) and *Kendallville, Indiana: Those Were the Days* (already a chapter title in this book, and one of the most-used nostalgia titles in print).

**Collision search (WebSearch, 25 Sep 2026):**
- "When We Walked Everywhere": no exact match found. Near misses: *Where Once We Walked* (Mokotoff and Sack, a guide to Jewish communities destroyed in the Holocaust; different shape and field but similar rhythm), *When We Walk By* (Adler and Burnes, on homelessness), *When We Go Walking* (Cari Best, picture book). None is a regional-history sibling.
- "One More Walk Through Town": no exact match found. Near miss that matters: *Walk Through Town* by Jim Smith, a hometown boyhood memoir of Tuscumbia, Alabama. Same genre, so it is a real sibling cost.
- "Loved That Place": no book with this title found. Nearby generic titles: *Hometown Memories* (Ken Tate; Brian Weaver; Thomas Kinkade).
- Existing Kendallville books to know about: Arcadia's *Kendallville and Noble County* (Postcard History Series) and *150 Years: Kendallville, Indiana* (Terry Housholder, KPC Media, 2013). Neither collides with any candidate; the Arcadia one is a reason to avoid the word "Kendallville and" as an opening.

## Merge and fold decisions

**(a) Mom Would Send Me There (Harman's IGA, 555 words) and That Little Store (corner groceries, 997 words): MERGE, proposed.**
Reasons: Harman's is thin on its own (the shortest chapter in the book), and the two chapters are built on the same device: both open with a child being sent for bread or bologna, both run on pop-bottle returns, both end on a building that is something else now. Printed side by side, the second would read as the first told again; printed apart, Harman's still has too little to stand on. The subject is one subject: where your mother sent you for groceries. That Little Store is already sectioned by store, so Harman's slots in without restructuring.
Exactly what would merge: insert a new section `## Harman's` into That Little Store after `## Diamond Street` and before `## Gone now`, carrying Mom Would Send Me There's paragraphs 2 to 9 (walking distance and bike rides; the two stores, Dowling Street then the A&P building at Riley and Wayne; clean store and carry-out boys; the food; phone booths and Jim's across the street; the high school jobs and the family affair behind the meat counter; the A&P bottle deposit; the downtown A&P and Kroger and the ground coffee). Drop its opening paragraph (the sending errand is already That Little Store's opening) and its "Loved that store" line. Move its closing line, "The building on Riley Street is a church now. It is still serving people, just not ham salad," into `## Gone now` beside Blue's and Ley's. Place line becomes *Riley Street Grocery, Blue's Market, Ley's, Harman's IGA and the corner stores*. Title stays **That Little Store** (its title phrase was measured on its own dump). Result about 1,450 words, within range of the longest chapters. The merged chapter needs a fresh verification pass on the joins before build.
Fallback if rejected: keep both, with Mom Would Send Me There immediately after That Little Store (as chapter 10), and cut its first paragraph to blunt the repeated opening.

**(b) It Was Magical (Christmas on Main, 578 words) and Walking Uptown (Main Street stores, 965 words): DO NOT MERGE; same part, bookend placement.**
Reasons: Christmas is a different subject (a season, the music, Silver Bells, the parade), and its closing image of walking beside your mother in the snow is one of the strongest in the book; folded into Walking Uptown it would become a paragraph and Walking Uptown would lose its own Saturday ending. Its Penney's material (the basement toy wonderland) complements rather than repeats Walking Uptown's Penney's (the balcony). Placed immediately after Walking Uptown, both chapters open on the same store-to-store walk and the second would feel like a rerun; so Walking Uptown opens Part II and It Was Magical closes it, and Main Street frames the part. The chapter already handles its two shadows correctly: Santa's Workshop is one deferring sentence ("that is the lake's story to tell"), and the old school's Christmas lights are a passing mention.

**(c) Chopping Town (drive-in stands) and Those Were the Days (cruising): NO RENAME NEEDED; placed fifteen chapters apart.**
The collision STATUS.md warned about was resolved when the cruising chapter took the title Those Were the Days; the phrase "Chopping Town" is backed on the drive-in chapter by 3 distinct commenters plus 5 other "chop" forms (drive-in-stands verification). Remaining risk: both chapters describe the loop, and the cruising chapter's line "we didn't cruise, we chopped town, get it right" will read as a callback to chapter 2's title. That is acceptable at this distance. Chopping Town goes in Part I (its body is the Redwood, Kelsey's and the DQ, food), Those Were the Days in Part IV (the night). Eating in the Caboose's "One end of the loop" section sits between them in Part I and is short enough not to crowd either.

**(d) Skate Night (Youth Center) and Bud and Maxine (bowling): NEIGHBORS, no merge.**
Both came out of the same youth-center dump and both are about 720 words, but they are two buildings, two sets of adults (Lois; Bud and Maxine Campbell) and two endings, and each is strong alone. Neighboring placement in Part III does the work. Bud and Maxine must come before the tornado chapter, which mentions the bowling alley in passing; it does (15 before 22).

**(e) Until They Closed the Doors (McCray plant and mansion), I Was Born There (McCray Memorial Hospital), The Kendallville Foundries: NO MERGE; ordered as a run in Part V.**
Three different subjects (refrigerators, iron, a hospital). The foundry and McCray chapters share the family-payroll device, so they are adjacent rather than scattered, where the echo reads as the town's pattern rather than a repeat. Order is Foundries, then McCray plant, then the hospital, because Until They Closed the Doors introduces Elmer McCray, and the hospital chapter then says the name came "after the McCray family gave so much to it": the reader already knows who that is.

**(f) Chronister's into Walking Uptown: DO NOT FOLD.**
The restaurants evaluator left this open. Folding the Chronister's section out would break After School and After the Bars, whose title and frame (a town's clock of places to sit down and be fed) depend on the after-school counter. The real overlap is green river and chocolate cokes at two soda fountains, Klinkenberg's in Walking Uptown and Chronister's here; they are two different counters and green river was the town's drink, so both can stand. After School sits at chapter 5, three chapters before Walking Uptown.

Cross-references run forward: the DQ's Bob Shook in chapter 2 before the Shooks' restaurant in chapter 7; Grubby and Harman's in chapter 1 before Harman's in chapter 9; Kelsey's in chapter 2 before the Texaco behind it in chapter 10; the Chuck Wagon in chapter 5 before the taverns chapter says "which is another chapter"; the bowling alley in chapter 15 before the tornado takes it in chapter 22.

**The closer.** One Last Walk-Thru ends the book because it lands the whole project's theme, not just its own subject: the town fought for the building and kept it, and the chapter's last turn is that keeping the building did not keep the thing. "The building made it through. That kid grew up a long time ago... You can have one last walk-thru. You just cannot walk back out at fourteen." That is the book's one sentence, said by the town about its own school.

### A Note Before You Begin

Kendallville is still on the map. You can park on Main Street and walk past the Strand, and it is still showing movies. You can call Jim's on Wayne Street on a Friday night and order it exactly the way your father did. The old school on Sheridan Street is still standing, and on Monday nights there are still kids skating at the Youth Center.

But the Kendallville in these pages is not quite that town. It was taken apart a piece at a time. Penney's closed, and Murphy's next door. The refrigerator plant closed its doors, stood empty for years and then burned. The foundries went quiet and were torn down. The hospital where most of the town was born came down, and a library stands on the spot. One corner store is a house now and another is a dental office, the drive-ins are gone, and the taverns closed. No single year did it. It went the way these things go, one closing at a time, until you could come home, stand in the middle of Main Street, and not find the town you grew up in.

Much of it is still standing. What is gone is the life that went on inside it: the Saturday walk from one store to the next, the tray at the car window, the man behind the counter who scolded you and then told you how the town used to be, the first job that seemed to be everybody's first job, and the kid you were in all of it.

This book rebuilds that town from the only material left, which is memory. It was put together from what the people who grew up there remember, in their own words, and checked wherever it could be checked. The names are real. The streets are real. Where people remember a thing differently, and they often do, the book says so rather than picking a winner.

It is not a history. There are not many dates in it. It is about where you ate, where you shopped, where you were sent for a loaf of bread, where you learned to swim, where you were born and where your father worked. The hope is a simple one: that somewhere in these pages you turn a corner and find your own childhood waiting there.

*The town could not keep these places. This book is one way of keeping them.*
