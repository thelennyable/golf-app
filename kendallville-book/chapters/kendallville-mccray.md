# Until They Closed the Doors
*The McCray Refrigerator plant and the McCray mansion*

Ask a Kendallville family whether their people worked at McCray's and you do not get a yes. You get a family tree. My dad. My dad and my two uncles and a pile of cousins. My grandpa, and my grandma too. A boy could start there at fourteen and stay until he retired. You could work the porcelain furnaces for the better part of half a century. You could meet your wife there, and your children could come along behind you and work in the same building. For generations of this town, the refrigerator plant was simply where the family worked.

It was Elmer McCray's company, and it built refrigerators, and it had room in it for nearly every kind of work a town could want. There was the M-line and line 5 and line 7, where you could learn electric wiring and industrial assembly from the ground up. There were the porcelain furnaces and the pattern shop, the welders and the foremen. There was the print shop, the drafting room, the switchboard, the punch cards for the computers. There was the office, and the advertising department, where you could stay for decades. You could drive semi for them and bring home green stamps.

For a kid out of high school it was the summer job. You met some of the most interesting characters of your life, and those characters were what made it a fun place to work. At quitting time the men ran for the time clock, and if your lunch pail had been quietly screwed to the workbench, you grabbed it on the run and it jerked you clean off your feet. It was always funny, right up until it happened to you. At noon the Dairy Queen scooter came down selling brazier burgers and shakes, and a tray went down on a rope from the second-floor windows to meet it.

By the late 1960s the company belonged to Litton Industries, and in the early seventies the refrigerator business closed its doors. That is the phrase the town uses, over and over, about fathers and uncles and themselves: worked there until they closed the doors. The sentence does not need anything added to it.

The building did not empty all at once. Other outfits moved into pieces of it. When it was Ayr-Way you built paddle boats and fiberglass truck caps under that roof. Later it was Colwell General, and a whole new generation of Kendallville worked there in the eighties and nineties, making color and texture swatches and running the tillbox machines. There were backstairs and passages to explore, and if you worked there then, you may wish you had explored more while you could.

Then it stood empty, and it went on standing empty. Trees came up inside it. If you had worked the line, looking at pictures of the inside was like looking at the Titanic after you had sailed on her. The city wanted it down. Some people wanted something smaller: a brick from it, when the time came. Then it burned, and the south end was still smoking hours afterward while people drove by to look. Gone for good now. All of our history is disappearing.

* * *

Elmer McCray's house still stands.

He built it in the 1920s, and it has been the town's great house ever since. Kids on Richmond Street grew up along its back fence, the black wrought iron one. You could grow up just down the street and never see the inside. If you did get in, even as a kid, it made an impression. It was magical. It looked so much bigger then.

Inside there is the staircase, and everyone who has been through the door starts with the staircase. There is a library, and a breakfast room, and up under the roof a ballroom where there were dances once. The wood spindles along the roof railing were turned on a lathe in the sixties. Most of those spindles are still up there. And in the kitchen there is a McCray cooler, built in, the original refrigeration, still running.

The house has belonged to a doctor's family, and it has been an inn, and it has held a great many of Kendallville's big nights. Weddings, and at least one reception with tents out back and a string quartet on the lawn and the cake at the bottom of the stairs. A rehearsal dinner. Anniversary parties. Company Christmas parties, where you toured the house before you ate. A graduation party, dinner theater, Christmas walks. If you trick-or-treated that side of town in the eighties, you might have come home with a roll of dimes instead of candy. The word the town reaches for, every time, is treasure.

There is not much left to remember Elmer McCray by. The plant where your grandfather and your father and maybe you yourself put in the hours is gone, burned and gone for good, and no brick you saved will put it back. But his house is still standing, cared for, and somewhere inside it, in a kitchen from the 1920s, one of his refrigerators is still keeping things cold.
