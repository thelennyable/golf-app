# Walking Uptown
*Main Street, Kendallville*

It was for everybody, which is the thing that is hardest to explain to anyone who did not grow up with it. It was for the family that came in from four miles west and the family that drove over from Rome City, for the kid with an allowance burning a hole in his pocket, for the mother with a list, for a girl and her best friend walking up on a Saturday with nothing to buy and all afternoon to not buy it in. You went uptown. On Saturday, you parked nose-in at a slant along Main Street, back when diagonal parking was simply how it was done, and you walked. Store to store, one side and then the other, and if you did it properly you could spend the whole day and eat lunch in the middle of it.

The anchor was Penney's. For a town the size of Kendallville it seemed enormous, a real department store with a real upstairs, and the upstairs is the first thing anyone mentions. It was a balcony, really, a half floor up at the back of the store, and it held whatever a child most wanted to get to. For years the children's clothes were up there, and you climbed those stairs for school clothes every single year before school started, because that was where school clothes came from. You looked down at the whole store laid out below you like a map. And if your grandmother decided the balcony was no place for a little boy, you stood at the bottom and looked up at it and wondered.

And then there was the money. Was it a cable and a trolley, a wire basket, a tube with a whoosh? Nobody quite agrees on the mechanism, and everybody agrees on the wonder. Children stood and watched it the way other children watched trains.

If you were small enough, that might be all you kept: not the merchandise, just the high ceilings and the hum of the fluorescent lights, the stairs. If you were a little older you kept more. Easter dresses. An Easter bonnet you pulled a face at and wore anyway because your mother insisted. The catalog counter, where you went to pick up what you had ordered from the big book at home. And it seemed as though everybody's mother, or grandmother, had worked the floor there at one time or another, in ready-to-wear or in the stockroom. It was a boy's first real job, stocking shelves and sweeping floors, under the store manager, the kind of boss a teenager on his first job does not forget.

Right next door was Murphy's, the dime store, and nobody went to one without drifting into the other. Murphy's was where an allowance went to die happy: on a Barbie doll, on a 45 record, on something from the candy counter, on whatever cheap wonderful thing had caught your eye the Saturday before. Penney's was where your mother took you. Murphy's was where you took yourself.

Farther along Main was Klinkenberg's, and if Penney's was where Kendallville bought its clothes, Klinkenberg's was where it sat down. It was an old drugstore gone gloriously out of time. There was a soda fountain with stools you were told to stop twirling on. There was the penny candy. There was a rack of comic books, and if you were a certain kind of kid you sat on the floor and read your way through them without buying a thing. Up front a nut warmer turned its slow circle, and there was a machine that dispensed peanuts, and you could drop a few into the bottom of your Coke.

Because the Coke is the point. Ask anybody what they drank at Klinkenberg's and you will get a flavor before you get a sentence. Chocolate Cokes, first and loudest. Green rivers, lime green and fizzing. Cherry Cokes, vanilla Cokes, phosphates, root beer floats, the best malted milk you ever had. A cherry Coke with a dirty spoon, if you like your memories honest. The whole room smelled of cigar smoke.

The man behind the counter was Bob Klinkenberg, and the town has never settled whether he was a grouch or a treasure. It is entirely possible he was both. He would scold you for reading the comics without paying, and then, if you sat long enough, he would start telling you how Kendallville used to be, and you would sit there with your green river going warm and not want him to stop.

And for shoes, if you had narrow feet, there was really only Connie's. Other stores had shoes; Connie Evers had your size, the double A, the skinny ankle, the one that fit nowhere else. Your mother took you there because it was the only place that could fit you, and Connie knew your name, and if you were lucky you walked out in saddle shoes.

It is hard to say exactly when it ended, because it did not end all at once. The town still argues about who killed it: Walmart, Fort Wayne, the chains that pulled out and left a hole where the national advertising used to be. What everyone agrees on is the feeling. Murphy's closed. Penney's closed, and Main Street has not been the same since. Klinkenberg's hung on longer than anyone had a right to expect, and the building still serves sweets today, a coffee and cake shop.

Walk into the old Penney's building now and you will not be able to fathom how small it is. It was the biggest store in the world. You climbed to the balcony, you walked out onto a street full of people you knew, and you had the whole of Saturday still ahead of you.
