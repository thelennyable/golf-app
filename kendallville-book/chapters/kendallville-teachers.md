# Don't Say Yes and Mean No
*The teachers of East Noble and the Kendallville schools*

Everybody who grew up in Kendallville carries a few sentences around that they did not write. They were handed over at a desk, or from a podium in the band room, or in a hallway between bells, by a grown-up who had probably forgotten saying them by supper. Fifty years later the sentences are still there. Some of them are the reason you finished school. A few of them are the reason you nearly didn't.

Ask who mattered and the names come back in bunches, the way roll used to be called. Bottomley. Munk. Abbs. Rickey. Schippers. Bentz. Nelson. Inniger. Swartzlander. Savage. Berhalter. Walters. Mrs. Black. Almost nobody can stop at one, and the standing joke is that anyone who lists more than five was a nerd. Most people will cheerfully own up to it.

Start in the band room, because so much of the town seems to start there. Pete Bottomley led East Noble music for a generation, and if you marched for him you know the man: tough as nails, demanding perfection again and again through all those days and nights of practice, and loving you the whole time. Pete and Jim, Bottomley and Swartzlander, come out in one breath, like salt and pepper. If your class toilet-papered his house senior year, that was love too.

Down the hall was the stage. Craig Munk, Chip to the kids who loved him, taught art at East Noble and ran its theatre, and for decades a shy, insecure kid could walk into his world and come out reaching for the stars. You went in to paint flats and came out with a solo.

Then there was math, which for a lot of Kendallville was the hard country. Bill Abbs had been a principal and would be the attendance officer for the whole system, but he is remembered at a desk with a piece of chalk, patient with the kid who could not get it, and saying the thing everybody who had him can still recite: *Don't say yes and mean no.* It sounded like a math rule. It turned out to be a life rule. You might also get *Is it clear, or clear as mud?*, and if you were lucky you saw him come across the parking lot in his Corvair station wagon with his foot already out the door. Fred Inniger taught math and then became the counselor, and there are grown adults who will tell you flatly that they graduated from East Noble because of him.

English meant Mrs. Black, and Mrs. Black meant Chaucer. *Whan that Aprille with his shoures soote.* People who have not opened a poem since can still reel off the prologue to *The Canterbury Tales* in the old tongue, because she made them, and they are glad. If you had her, April 23 is still Shakespeare's birthday in your head and 1066 was her license plate. Later there was Mrs. Bentz reading the same Chaucer aloud until the laughing stopped and the listening started, and daring the class to finish *Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance*. Mr. Rickey would bet you a bag of M&M's you could not be quiet for fifteen minutes. Mrs. Schippers told you to pull yourself up by your own bootstraps, because nobody else was going to do it for you, and there was a day she drank her tea out of her own high-heeled shoe because a boy who wanted no part of school got the answer right.

Mrs. Rhodes scared the living daylights out of biology students and taught them more than anyone; if you were in her room the January morning the Challenger came apart, you remember her walking in crying and wheeling in the television. In chemistry the word was *moles*, and nobody could pronounce it quite like Miss Dearth.

And not every lesson happened at a desk. Carl Pulver taught welding, Harold Charles taught what an automobile actually is, Jim Stanchfield ran the machine shop, and Zippy had the wood shop and a reputation as about as tough, fair and honest as anyone in the building. Men still make their living on what was taught in those rooms. For a farm kid there was ag and FFA, and the teacher who drove out to your place to talk your parents into letting you go.

Before any of that, there was the elementary school. A kindergarten teacher handed you your first love of reading. Barb Kelley at Avilla started the day with a prayer and made history come alive, and if you had her and grew up to teach, you may have spent your career trying to be a Mrs. Kelly to another kid.

Then there are the other sentences.

*It's obvious you're not the student your sister was.* There is no end to the younger brothers and sisters in this town who heard some version of that on the first day of class, measured against an older sibling before they had opened a book. *I sure hope you're as smart as your brother.* A guidance counselor could look at a thirteen-year-old and advise a general course and a good job in a factory. You could be told you would never amount to anything, or that you would be in prison before twenty-one, or that nothing good came out of the south end of town. You could ask a math teacher for help and be told to go back and read the book. There was the ruler across the knuckles, the flick of a red fingernail on the back of the head, the chalkboard eraser thrown hard enough to sting. At football practice there was no water, just run faster and hit the sled, and maybe a salt pill.

The words stayed. So, it turns out, did the answer. Two master's degrees. A doctorate. An associate professor with three degrees. Twenty years of running your own business. Twenty-three years as a police officer, twelve of them as the chief. The strange gift of a mean teacher is that every teacher teaches you something, even if it is only what not to do, and if you grew up to stand in front of a classroom yourself, you were determined not to pass it on.

Their words have power. The good ones knew it, and spent it well.

The funny thing is that the teachers mostly don't remember you. A high school teacher sees a hundred and fifty kids a semester, and years later a grey-bearded grandfather walks up and gives a name, and the teacher smiles and has no idea. It doesn't matter. You remember them. You remember the exact wording.

A lot of those names live in obituaries now. The band has had other directors, the classrooms other voices, and the building has gone on turning out kids who will carry sentences of their own. You cannot go back and thank them all. But somewhere in Kendallville, every day, a grown kid who once sat in those rooms answers yes, and means it.
