# Don't Say Yes and Mean No
*The teachers of East Noble and the Kendallville schools*

Everybody who grew up in Kendallville carries a few sentences around that they did not write. They were handed over at a desk, or from a podium in the band room, or in a hallway between bells, by a grown-up who had probably forgotten saying them by supper. Fifty years later the sentences are still there. Some of them are the reason you finished school. A few of them are the reason you nearly didn't.

Ask who mattered and the names come back in bunches, the way roll used to be called. Bottomley. Munk. Abbs. Rickey. Schippers. Bentz. Nelson. Inniger. Swartzlander. Savage. Berhalter. Walters. Mrs. Black. Almost nobody can stop at one.

Start in the band room, because so much of the town seems to start there. Pete Bottomley led East Noble music for a generation, and if you marched for him you know the man: tough as nails, demanding perfection again and again through all those days and nights of practice, and loving you the whole time. Pete and Jim, Bottomley and Swartzlander, come out in one breath, like salt and pepper.

Down the hall was the stage. Craig Munk, Chip to the kids who loved him, taught art at East Noble and ran its theatre.

Then there was math, which for a lot of Kendallville was the hard country. Bill Abbs had been a principal and would be the attendance officer for the whole system, but he is remembered at a desk with a piece of chalk, patient with the kid who could not get it, and saying *Don't say yes and mean no.* It sounded like a math rule. It turned out to be a life rule. Fred Inniger taught math and then became the counselor, and there are grown adults who will tell you that they graduated from East Noble because of him.

English meant Mrs. Black, and Mrs. Black meant Chaucer. *Whan that Aprille with his shoures soote.* Later there was Mrs. Bentz daring the class to finish *Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance*.

And not every lesson happened at a desk. Carl Pulver taught welding, Harold Charles taught what an automobile actually is, and Jim Stanchfield ran the machine shop. Men still make their living on what was taught in those rooms. For a farm kid there was ag and FFA, and the teacher who drove out to your place to talk your parents into letting you go.

Before any of that, there was the elementary school. A kindergarten teacher handed you your first love of reading.

Then there are the other sentences.

*It's obvious you're not the student your sister was.* There is no end to the younger brothers and sisters in this town who heard some version of that, measured against an older sibling. *I sure hope you are as smart as your brother.* You could be told you would never amount to anything, or that you would be in prison before twenty-one, or that nothing good came out of the south end of town. You could ask a math teacher for help and be told to go back and read the book. There was the ruler across the knuckles, the flick of a red fingernail on the back of the head, the chalkboard eraser thrown. At football practice there was no water, just run faster and hit the sled, and maybe a salt pill.

The words stayed. So, it turns out, did the answer. Two master's degrees. An associate professor with three degrees. Twenty years of running your own business. Twenty-three years as a police officer, twelve of them as the chief. The strange gift of a mean teacher is that every teacher teaches you something, even if it is only what not to do, and if you grew up to stand in front of a classroom yourself, you were determined not to pass it on.

Their words have power. The good ones knew it, and spent it well.

The funny thing is that the teachers mostly don't remember you. Years later a grey-bearded grandfather walks up and gives a name, and the teacher has no idea. It doesn't matter. You remember them. You remember the exact wording.

A lot of those names live in obituaries now. The band has had other directors, the classrooms other voices, and the building has gone on turning out kids who will carry sentences of their own. You cannot go back and thank them all. But somewhere in Kendallville, every day, a grown kid who once sat in those rooms answers yes, and means it.
