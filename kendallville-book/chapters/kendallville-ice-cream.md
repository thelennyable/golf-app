# The Best Ice Cream
*The Palace of Sweets and Puritan Ice Cream*

For a long stretch of Kendallville's life, something sweet came from one of two directions. If you were a teenager with a little money in your pocket, you went uptown to the Palace of Sweets, to the booths and the jukebox and the man behind the counter. If you were a kid with your family, or on your bike, or walking home from school, you went to the ice cream plant, which was Puritan's to everyone until the name on it became Atz. Two places, two different ages of the same childhood.

## The Palace

The Palace of Sweets was where you went after school. You went on a Saturday afternoon, and you went before the movie and after it. It was where you celebrated the last day of school. Every booth had its own little record box, a jukebox at the table, and you fed it quarters. It may well be the first place you ever heard the Beatles. There was a pinball machine, five balls for a nickel, and if you were a girl you did not get much of a turn at it, because the boys had it.

What you ordered depended on what you had. French fries and a Coke. A cherry Coke, which was, for a certain crowd, a big topic. A burger, a cheeseburger, a milkshake, hot fudge over the ice cream. You could eat a burger, fries and a shake for 97 cents and still have change left for candy or pop if you wanted to see a movie.

The businessmen came in for coffee breaks and the lunch hour was crowded, and the kitchen turned out the bread pudding, the ham loaf and the baked beans, creamed chicken and biscuits, and, if memory serves, potato soup on Thursdays in the winter. And there was the candy, because the man who ran the place was a candy maker too. He made his own chocolates in the basement, and a glazed maple candy.

That man was Kenny Blech, and he owned the Palace for many years. Ask about him and the first word you get is grumpy, and the next word is always but. He was grumpy when you were too loud. He was grumpy when you ran from booth to booth. He would yell, and if you broke one of his rules he would put you out. And then: a heart of gold, a huge heart for the teenagers, a kind man under all of it, a good man, a one-of-a-kind. Plenty of people who went there do not remember him being grumpy at all. The ones who do tend to take his side now. The place was usually packed with loud kids, wild kids, punk teenagers who drove everyone crazy, and looking back, you would have been grumpy too.

For a lot of Kendallville teenagers the Palace was also a job, and some of them had been customers first. It was a great place to work, and he was a wonderful boss who treated his help right. You can still hear the back door slam shut.

## Puritan's

The other place was not uptown. It was a factory in a neighborhood, and the neighborhood knew it. Kids grew up across the street from it, around the corner from it, a block away, down the road. There was a school right behind it, and you stopped on your way home. Families walked over. You rode your bike there. Classes came on field trips.

The first thing was the smell when you went in: cool and sweet, the smell of ice cream, and you can smell it still. When Puritan had its retail store there were small white wire chairs with red hearts in the backrests. Through the big window you could watch them making the ice cream.

And then the flavors. Bubblegum. Baseball nut. Pistachio. Moose tracks. Lemon, and coffee. And dill pickle, which is not a joke, or not only a joke, because you walked there on purpose to get it. There were turtle sundaes and banana splits, chocolate malts and cherry floats, nickel cones in the fifties, half pints to take home. A banana boat was fifty cents, a double for a dollar. There were the sheet pan ice cream squares, decorated for a shower or a birthday, which you had probably forgotten about until just now. You could buy baseball cards there, too.

It was a workplace as much as a treat. A lot of Kendallville families had one of their own on the payroll: a father driving one of the big trucks that took the ice cream out to the stores; an older brother who always came home with ice cream. The Atzes, by every account, were good people to work for. You could start there under one name and finish under the other.

## After

The Palace is Jeny's Tacos now. The ice cream plant is gone altogether, torn down, and senior apartments stand where it was.

That is how it goes with places like these. The building was never really the point. The point was being fifteen in a booth with a cherry Coke and a quarter for the jukebox, or being a kid who had ridden over on a bike, deciding whether you were brave enough for dill pickle. Ask anybody who went to either one and you will get the same few words, the only review either place ever needed. Loved that place.
