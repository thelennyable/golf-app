# That Little Store
*Riley Street Grocery, Blue's Market, Ley's and the corner stores*

Every Kendallville neighborhood had its store, and every kid in it knew the way. It was close enough that you could be sent: for a loaf of bread and a pound of bologna, for cold meat and popsicles, for whatever your grandmother needed that day, because every day, it never failed, she needed something. And it was close enough that you could go on your own, on foot or on your bike, with a nickel or a quarter, and come home with a bag full of candy.

There were more of them than anyone can keep straight now. Riley Street had one. South Main had one. West William had one, and Diamond Street, and Iddings, and Mitchell, and the list grows every time the town starts talking about it. Ask who ran the little store on Diamond Street and you will get three family names and an argument that never quite ends. What nobody argues about is the candy.

## Riley Street

The one most people mean is Riley Street Grocery, which the Haskins ran, Dick and Marge, and they were always so nice to the kids. It sat a short walk from the schools. You went up at lunchtime for pop and candy or a fresh made sandwich. You rode your bike down to pick up things for your mother. You played basketball at the school and cooled off with a pop from the machine out front. You went for baseball cards. You went for penny candy, and it came in a brown paper bag.

Once you got to junior high, it was a big deal to skip lunch period and go to Riley Street Grocery, and then get back in time for the next class. The lunch money was meant for a proper school lunch. It went instead on a Hostess cherry pie and a Coke, eaten on the walk back to school, and if your father happened to drive past just then, the only thing you could think to do was wave.

## Blue's

On the south end it was Blue's Market on South Main, and before that it was South Side Market, and to plenty of people it never stopped being South Side Market, or simply Tommy's. Tom Johnson had it for years. Then Larry and Dick Blue had it, and the town, inevitably, called them the Blues Brothers.

It was a grocery, but what Blue's really was, was a meat counter and a sandwich. The meat was cut fresh in front of you, and the south end will still tell you it was the best in town. The sandwiches were made to order out of the case, the way you wanted them, huge and loaded with meat: roast beef and Colby, ham and turkey with Colby and lettuce and mayo, the best bologna sandwich around. In the nineties it was a dollar forty-nine. Somehow a sandwich from Blue's was always better than the same sandwich made at home.

At noon the factories came over. Men from Reliable, which sat right behind the store, and from the foundries, and from Dalton and King Seeley, walked down for a sandwich, and you could walk back to work eating yours. If you got off at Newman's on a Friday afternoon, you could cash your check at Blue's and buy a few of their subs. On the side there was ham salad, ham spread, the one macaroni salad you ever actually liked, and a bag of hot chips with a Mountain Dew.

For the kids, Blue's ran on bottles. Your mother sent you over with her returnable Pepsi bottles and enough money, almost to the penny, for a Pepsi, a loaf of bread and a pound of bologna. Anything left over went to candy. You turned in your own bottles for another pop and a candy bar, or for comics, or ice cream. If you were small and slow at picking out penny candy, the tall lady at the register let you take all the time you needed. And you still know the smell it had when you walked in the front door.

## Ley's

Out on the west end the store was Ley's, on West William Street, and for the west-end kids it was the school bus stop. You caught the bus there for kindergarten, first, second and third grade. On the cold days they had you come inside to warm up. On your paper route, on a bitter night, with frozen feet and hands, Ley's was a beacon of warmth halfway through, a place to waste a little of your hard-earned money before heading home. If the bread truck pulled up while you waited for the bus, the driver might hand you a slice of fresh, soft white bread. And there was always penny candy: black jack squares, candy logs, candy cigarettes, a sweet treat if you had a nickel, a small sackful if you had a quarter.

The wooden floors squeaked. If you lived out in the country, Ley's might be where the whole family did the once-a-week shopping on a Friday or Saturday night, or where you stopped with your mom or dad on the way home from the co-op feed mill.

## Diamond Street

The store on Diamond Street is the one everyone half-knows and nobody can name to the room's satisfaction. It had a big porch, and the big kids hung out on it, on the porch and the steps. You bought penny candy there, and popsicles, and blackjack toffee chews, and gum after school. You may even have slipped out the back door of the auditorium to buy your treats there.

For a certain kind of small child, it was the first store you were ever allowed to walk to by yourself. You were four, and you went for candy. Imagine letting a four-year-old head out alone for candy in this day and age.

At Ley's, too, you could make that first trip by yourself and never know your dad was following you the whole way.

## Gone now

The south end will tell you Blue's lost its lunch crowd when the factories shut down. It closed, and the building at 900 South Main is a dental office now; if you ever ordered a roast beef there, a root canal in the same room takes some getting used to. You can live across the street from the old Ley's building today and never know there was a little shop there; the porch is gone, and it doesn't look like the same building. The store on Diamond is a house.

Every neighborhood should have a store like that. They are all gone now. What is left is a bag of penny candy you could fill for a quarter, a sandwich that weighed a pound, and the walk there, which was the first walk you ever took on your own.
