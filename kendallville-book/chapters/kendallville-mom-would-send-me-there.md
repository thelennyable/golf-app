# Mom Would Send Me There
*Harman's IGA*

This one belongs to the kids who got sent. Mom would send you there for bread. Mom would send you there for milk, or eggs, or whatever she forgot, and if your family had a dollar for every time it sent a kid to that store for something forgotten, it would be rich. Sometimes it was cigarettes, Winstons in the red pack.

The place you got sent was Harman's IGA. It was walking distance. It was a short walk from houses on Riley Street and Moyer Street, a trip to pick up groceries for Grandma, close enough that some families lived right next door. It was a bike ride from everywhere else. You might have pedaled over from the campground at Bixler Lake. You might have just ridden your bike around the parking lot.

There were two Harman's. The first was the smaller store over on Dowling Street. Then Virgil Harman moved into the A&P building on Riley Street at Wayne. Mr. Harman treated everybody like family, and the Harmans were good people.

It was always nice and clean. It was a store where a carry-out boy still carried your groceries out for you, and when you had small children, that was a kindness you did not forget.

Then there was the food, and the food is where the memories stop being polite and start being hungry. The donuts, glazed and yeast, were the bomb. The ham salad was the best ever. The rotisserie chicken was the best chicken for takeout. The potato wedges were so crispy. There were pizza pockets, or pizza puffs, and in the summer you could go there for lunch and get one. They were the best pizza pockets ever. There was onion dip. There were peanut clusters with filling, in those white bags.

There were phone booths out front. It sat across from Jim's Pizza, and if you worked at Jim's you could run across the street, buy a TV dinner and heat it up in the pizza oven.

A lot of Kendallville worked there in high school. Kids ran the checkouts all through high school. Kids carried out groceries. It might have been your first job, and it was a lot of fun. Behind the meat counter, which was a great meat counter, stood some of the town's fathers and a good number of the town's mothers too. Mothers worked the checkout for years, some at the old store and the new one both. A mother could be in the meat department while her daughter ran a register up front. It was a family affair.

Before it was Harman's, the Riley Street building was the A&P. You could take your empty pop bottles back there and get ten cents a bottle.

And before all of that, there were the stores downtown. In the fifties there was an A&P and a Kroger on Main Street. What sticks is the coffee: you could grind the beans yourself and put the coffee in a bag, and the whole store smelled so good. Ice cream from the A&P downtown was such a treat that you remember helping your mother buy it.

Loved that store. Sure miss this store. Wish they were still open.

The building on Riley Street is a church now. It is still serving people, just not ham salad.
