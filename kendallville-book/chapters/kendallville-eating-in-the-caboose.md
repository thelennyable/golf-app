# Eating in the Caboose
*Burger Chef, Kendallville*

If you were a kid in Kendallville then, Burger Chef was not really a place to eat. It was a place to eat in the caboose. That was the whole negotiation from the back seat. If you had boys, they always wanted to go there, and they wanted to sit in the caboose. If you were the kid, you did not merely want to. You HAD to. Mothers brought their children. Fathers brought their sons. You went with your grandpa, or your grandma, and the burgers were good, but the burgers were never the point. The point was the train car.

It was a real caboose, attached to the restaurant, a dining car you could sit down in. It was sturdy, which was a mercy, because kids tore it up all day. It was cold in the winter. You could read Burger Chef and Jeff comics in it, and when you were older you could load up your burger and smoke a cigarette in it. Getting out to eat was the treat, and lunch in a train car was the best treat of all.

Birthdays happened in there. You had one of your birthday parties in the caboose, or you went to a friend's. There was a hostess to run it, and there could be a cake shaped like a train. And if you grew up and moved away, you might stop in when visiting your mom and dad, so your own kids could sit in it too.

## The fixin' bar

What you ordered was, at first, almost nothing: a plain hamburger. Then you carried it to the fixin' bar and made it yours. The Burger Works, the fixin's bar, the fixin' table; whatever you called it, you loaded up. You could take a plain hamburger and build it two feet high. It was entirely possible to walk out having ordered a burger and gotten everything but the meat. There are grown people who still talk about ten-cent burgers, and about buying them cheap and loading them up.

Out front, the marquee made an offer that still gets a WOW: *Try our salad bar today. All you can eat. 79¢.* It was a great salad bar, the best in town, maybe the best ever. There was the Big Chef, which you can still find yourself craving, and the Super Chef, and a hot ham and Swiss that nobody has had better since. And there was the kids' meal, the Fun Meal, which came in a box that was more like an activity box, with Burger Chef and Jeff on it, a cheap plastic record inside, and a wrapped sandwich cookie for dessert that was SOOOO good.

## One end of the loop

For the teenagers it was something else again. When you chopped town in the seventies, Burger Chef was one end of the circuit, the place where you turned around, looping around the back past the drive-through lane and heading back through town. It was the hangout. It was where you went after church on a Sunday, and where you went after the basketball game.

After one of those games, in the seventies, the fixin' table became ammunition. Rival fans started throwing everything on it, the salad bar included, the police were called, and Burger Chef was shut down fast and everyone was asked, not politely, to leave. If you were there, you may have left before the police got there.

It was also, for a stretch, the place for streakers. A car would drop one off at one door; the streaker would run straight through and out the other door into the same car, which had circled around. At least once it was done in nothing but a stocking cap. It stopped, as the story goes, only when they locked one of the doors.

The town had a name for it, too: Barf and Choke. And if you ever worked the counter and greeted a customer with "Welcome to Barf and Choke" and the manager overheard, you were canned.

## The first job for so many

Because the other thing Burger Chef was, for a whole generation of Kendallville teenagers, was work. It was a first job, the first job for so many, the first one other than babysitting. At first the crew wore brown, and then came the caboose suit, a striped apron and an engineer's cap. Fun times, and busy ones, and if you worked there you may well have been the one stuck cleaning that caboose at the end of the day.

## Where the caboose went

As the town tells it, this was the DQ's first real competition, the first chain fast-food restaurant Kendallville had apart from the drive-ins, and when it came the party moved. Nobody agrees on the year the caboose arrived. You might have left town before it came, or been working the counter the day it was brought in.

Burger Chef itself was an Indiana chain, and in 1982 it was sold to the company that owned Hardee's. In Kendallville the sign changed more than once after that, and the order is still argued over: O'Dell's in there somewhere, then Hardee's, then Arby's. Hardee's kept the caboose. Arby's did not.

The building, the town will tell you, is an Arby's now. The caboose was moved, and it did not go far. It sits out on State Road 3, south of Avilla, on the east side of the road, where you can see it from the car as you drive by. Too bad kids can't experience it today. You can slow down when you pass it. What you cannot do is be small again, in the back seat, asking whether you can please sit in the caboose.
