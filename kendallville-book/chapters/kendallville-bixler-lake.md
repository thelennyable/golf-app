# We Lived at the Lake
*Bixler Lake*

It was for the kids who had nowhere to be from June to August. The ones with a bath towel slung over one shoulder and an inner tube hooked on the handlebars, pedaling down through town in the heat with the whole day in front of them. It was for the kid who turned eleven and was finally allowed to go without an older brother or sister along, and for the grandchildren who came to Kendallville and got walked down to the water. It was for everybody, and it cost nothing, and the town had it right there inside the city limits: a real lake, with a real beach.

Ask anyone who grew up in Kendallville where they spent their summers and the answer comes back in almost the same words every time. We lived at the lake. From sun up to sun down. Till way past dusk. It was a second home. You could be there from the age of four until you were grown, every nice day, all summer, and never once wonder where else you might go.

## Sink or swim

This is where Kendallville learned to swim. Ask around and the hands go up, decade after decade, from the late 1940s on: learned to swim there, learned to swim there, me too. The lessons were in the morning, and the morning water at Bixler was cold. You stood in it shivering with your arms wrapped around yourself, and the teacher was patient, and it might take you two summers to get it. Some kids got the older-sibling method instead, a shove off the end of the pier and a cheerful "sink or swim," and lived to tell it.

By noon the beach was full. If you did not get there early, there was nowhere left to spread your towel. Back then the road still ran right along the beach.

## The tower and the slide

What you remember depends on your decade. Some remember a long pier running out into the lake with a tower and springboards on either side of it. Later there was a raft you swam out to, with a low board, a high board and a slide. Nobody quite agrees on which came when, and it does not matter. Every version had the same thing at the top of it: the high dive, and you, standing on it.

It could take you a long time to jump. Some never did, and climbed back down the ladder while the line behind them had to back up and let them through. You might jump at eight years old, and then your brother had to go too, because now there was no choice. And some went off it wrong and hit the water flat on their faces.

Every so often the whistle blew and the lifeguard called "Family Check!" If you were a kid, those minutes lasted forever. If you were the lifeguard, there were never enough of them. For a long run of Kendallville teenagers that chair was a summer job, two seasons, three, four, five, and if you were down in the water you might well have had a crush on whoever was sitting in it.

## Frozen Zero bars

Across the road, up in the park, stood the beach house, a bath house with a concession stand, and the concession stand is where the day got its sugar. A pop and a candy bar. Grape gumballs. And above everything else, the Zero bars, the white-coated candy bars kept in the freezer, so on a hot afternoon you got one frozen hard and ate it dripping on the sand. Bring up the beach with anyone from that era and the Zero bars arrive within a minute. They are still the taste of that place.

There were afternoon park activities too, crafts and kickball and swim days, and the park's summer day camp. But the lake was the center of it.

## The workshop by the boat launch

The lake had a winter season too, and it lived in a garage.

Down near the boating area, one house turned every December into Santa's Workshop. George Workman built it: elves moving, working, a whole animated shop set up inside his garage, and a sleigh with Santa and the reindeer flying over the rooftop outside. He put it up for the Fourth of July as well, but Christmas was the one the town came for.

You cruised Kendallville looking at Christmas lights, and then you went to the lake and drove past Mr. Workman's slowly, or pulled over and sat in the car and just watched. You went more than once a season, because every time there was something you had missed the last time. And if he saw children out there, he came out, and let them walk right up to the garage, and told them how he made the elves. People who went as children took their own children, and then their grandchildren.

And then one year the garage stayed closed. You still think of it when you pass that house.

## Not a single person swimming

Walk around Bixler on a summer afternoon now and you might not see one swimmer. The high dive is gone, and the slide, and most of what stood out on the water; the raft is still there. The town argues about why, and the argument never ends. It was insurance, or it was liability. It is the geese and what they leave on the sand. It is the water, which gets tested every week and closed the day a test comes back high, and reopened when it clears. Others will tell you the beach is busy plenty of days and the lake is the jewel of the town, and they are not wrong either.

None of that is really what the argument is about. What people are really asking is where it went: the full beach at noon, the kid on the high board who has not decided yet. Those summers are still out there on the water somewhere. You just cannot swim out to them anymore.
