# Bud and Maxine
*The Kendallville Bowling Center, North Park Avenue*

It was a place where the kids and the parents could enjoy themselves, and it took a lot of hard work to keep it that way. It stood just over the tracks on Park Avenue, and on a Saturday it belonged to the kids. Saturday mornings, it was the place to be. It was the bowling alley, and in the years most people mean when they talk about it, the sixties and the seventies, it was Bud and Maxine's.

Saturday meant time to bowl. You were a Saturday morning kid. You bowled in a kids' league, a junior league, and you bowled every Saturday during the school year from the time you were little. There was no real age limit; if you could throw the ball, you were old enough. The little ones bowled in the morning, in the bantam league, two games each. The junior high and high school kids came in the afternoon for three games, and in between, Bud wiped down the lanes. You paid three dollars for your three games. If you were left-handed and had no ball of your own, you threw the right-handed house balls, and you learned early that the difference is in where the finger holes are. You were on the same team with the same kids for several years, and those were the best of times.

Maxine took care of the Saturday leagues. She coached, she got you ready for tournaments, and she came around and encouraged every kid. There were trips to Fort Wayne to bowl against the Fort Wayne kids, and to tournaments in Auburn. You might come home with a trophy, and it might be the only bowling trophy you ever won. In high school your team might win its age group's championship in a hard-fought game, with a lot of people there to watch.

Ask anybody who bowled there as a kid and you get the same few words back. Loved Bud and Maxine. They were always so nice. Bud and Maxine were the best. If you bowled there long enough, the Campbells were family.

Bud found you a ball. Your first bowling ball of your own might have come from Bud, in high school, a turquoise and blue swirl, and you might still have been rolling it in a league somewhere else years later. If you threw it too hard, Bud got after you, and then he smiled and shook his head.

Then there was the counter. They made great milkshakes at the bowling alley, and the hamburger was good too. You sat up at the counter and ordered snacks and shakes, and you ran around the place giving Bud fits. Later on there was Space Invaders, and your first high score on it might have gone up there. If you lived around the corner on Dowling Street, the bowling alley was practically your own. And on a winter night, walking out along the railroad tracks with your brother, you could stop in just to warm up.

The grown-ups had their nights. Mothers and fathers bowled in leagues there, and you came along while they bowled. Fridays. A Thursday night league, with a team your dad might sponsor for years. The women had the Monday Night Sallies, and when you were old enough you could bowl beside your mother. The women's teams wore matching uniforms; one year it might be white skirts you made yourselves and the neatest green shirts. It was your night out, and afterward you went to Elmer's or the Elks, no husbands. There was a trip to Indianapolis for a bowling tournament, and needless to say, you did not win.

It did not start with the Campbells. Before them it was Aldrich Lanes, and after them it had other owners. On July 14, 1992, the tornado came through town, turned north at Park Avenue and took the roof off the bowling alley. The owner tore it down. It was a shocking day when they did, and you wish you had bought some of the bowling pins.

It was on the north side of the tracks, on the west side of the street. The ground where it stood became a lot where the little trailers were for sale. Nothing of the bowling alley is left to see. Saturday mornings. The counter and the shakes. The team you had for years. Maxine coming around to encourage every kid. Bud shaking his head and smiling. The building is gone. The Saturday morning kid you were is still in there somewhere.
