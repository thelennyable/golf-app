# One Last Walk-Thru
*The old Kendallville High School and Middle School, Sheridan Street*

Some buildings in a town belong to one generation. This one belonged to almost all of them. If you grew up in Kendallville, the odds are good you went through its doors at some point between five years old and fifteen or eighteen, and plenty of the town went through them for nearly the whole stretch: kindergarten in the same brick pile where an older brother was sitting out a study hall, the middle school years in the halls a parent had graduated from. It was Kendallville High School, and after the last class graduated in 1966 it was the elementary and then the middle school, and for the kids who lived close it was simply the school. K through 8 under one roof, and then on to East Noble.

Most of them walked. The streets around it were full of families who lived a block away, two blocks, two doors down, straight across Sheridan Street from the gym doors. You came down the alley in the morning and went home the same way, past the tall smokestack behind the building, and after school you went home with the same kids you would walk with the next morning, and some of them are friends still.

And you went in by the K door. There was a big K above it, and ask anybody who went there how they got into the building and that is the answer. When it looked like the whole place might come down, you would have settled, if it came to that, for keeping the door.

## Where the building put you

Inside, the building told you how old you were by where it put you. For a long stretch of years kindergarten was down in the basement, down a lot of steps, and you could be five years old walking down all those stairs to a room below ground and feel, for the first time in your life, grown up. There was a little glass bottle of milk and a cookie.

If you came in as a freshman in the high school years, the freshman study hall was on the top floor, up a wide stairway and then a double set of stairs. On the first day you sat there and watched the room fill one kid at a time, every new face getting the once-over from the ones already seated, and the new kids coming in from Wayne Center getting it twice. Out the door and down the hall was the much bigger senior study hall, its main entrance across from the principal's office. The whole of high school in one corridor.

There was the auditorium, where you might have played your flute on the stage and sung your first solo, and where you came back for programs long after you had graduated. And there was the heat. On an early fall afternoon the upper floors could be an oven. You opened the windows, which had no screens, and your teacher warned the whole room not to throw anything out of them. The lower part of the building was cooler, and the floor down there ramped, and you liked it for that. In the last years you could be walking between classes and watch a ceiling tile come down in the middle of the hallway.

## The gym on Sheridan Street

If the building had a heart, though, it was the gym, the one your father might still have called the new gym long after it was not. The alley ran along its north end. The baskets hung at the north and south ends and the seats rose along the east and west sides, and at the south end there was a balcony, where you might remember the pep band, and a popcorn machine in the corner. Brought along as a little kid, you did not watch much basketball; you walked around the top of the gym and went for pop and popcorn.

These were the Kendallville Comets, and at sectional time the cars were parked up and down Sheridan Street. There was a scoreboard worked by hand over the north entrance, and a kid up there flipping the numbers and trying to keep up with the electric one in the corner. If you dressed out in the rooms under the bleachers, you can probably still smell them.

In the middle school years it was where you practiced. Practice let out around half past five and you walked home in the dark. If you lived close enough, there were weekends and holidays when the gym was empty and you found your way in and had the whole floor to yourself. If you have ever watched *Hoosiers* and thought, that is my gym, this is probably the gym you meant.

## The fight

By the time the students left, the building was old in all the ways that cost money: the heat, the pipes, three stories and no easy way to make them fit the rules for a modern school. In 2018 the middle school moved out to a new building on Drake Road, and before the year was out the school board voted to tear the old one down.

The town did not take it quietly. It argued the way towns argue. Apartments, or not apartments. A museum. A boys and girls club. A skate park. A place for kids to play ball in the summer. It would cost a fortune to heat, and everybody knew it, and nobody could stand the thought of an empty lot on Sheridan Street. Underneath every idea was the same wish, over and over, in half a dozen different ways: to walk those halls one more time. To see the kindergarten room again. To go back for twenty-four hours.

This is, after all, the school that turned out Harold Urey, who won the Nobel Prize in chemistry, and Arthur Franklin Mapes, who became Indiana's poet laureate.

And the town kept it. The school board signed it over to the city, the Dekko Foundation put money behind it, and in 2019 it opened again as the Community Learning Center: not a school, but a place for anybody of any age to come and exercise, learn a trade, take up a craft or look for a job.

## What is still in there

So you can go in now. The brick is still red against a blue sky, and the door on Sheridan Street still opens.

But the thing you want back is not really the building. It is the kid who came down the alley with a lunch and went in under the big K without thinking about it, who sat in the top-floor study hall waiting to be looked over, who walked home in the dark from practice. The building made it through. That kid grew up a long time ago and moved on, to East Noble or somewhere farther, and nobody could save a room for that.

You can have one last walk-thru. You just cannot walk back out at fourteen.
