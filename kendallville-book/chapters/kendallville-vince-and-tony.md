# Vince and Tony
*The V&A Restaurant, and after it the Jade Buddha*

It was the place to go for dinner. It was the place to eat in the fifties, the high end eatery when you were a kid, the upscale spot you might have always wanted to try and never quite did. It stood on Main Street by the railroad tracks, and it was called the V&A: Vince and Anthony, the Carunchia brothers. Vince and Tony, to the town.

It was for everybody, at every hour. Families went on Sundays. If you were a kid in the sixties, you loved the Friday night buffet, and the Saturday night buffets were standing room only, with people in from Fort Wayne and the towns around for the food. Workers from Flint & Walling ate lunch there several times a week, some of them every day. It had a lot of regulars. Your father might have ordered the same lunch every weekday for years, and the only decision left was which pie. It could be your morning coffee, or your every-morning breakfast stop for many, many years.

## The kitchen

Vince was the cook, and an amazing one. He would come out of the kitchen with his apron on and a cigar in the corner of his mouth, and go table to table asking if the food was all right. Nearly everything was made from scratch, and nothing was wasted. Very few restaurants make good homemade food. This one did.

The best burgers, and the garbage burger. The creamed chicken. Veal parmesan. There was a stretch when a dollar bought all the spaghetti and meatballs you could eat. And there was pizza, which in the fifties was a big deal all by itself. It may have been the first pizza you ever had, brought home by your father, messy in the good way, eaten with a knife and fork.

Then dessert, which is where the V&A really lives. The cream pies stood so high. Remember how high? Chocolate, lemon, so many. And the bread pudding. Vince was famous for it. Bring up the V&A and, sooner or later, it is the bread pudding. Best bread pudding ever. That famous bread pudding. The great bread pudding at the V&A.

## The help

The boys bused tables and washed dishes. You might have made two dollars an hour at it, and Vince might have pitched in at the sink beside you. It was a job you took in high school, or the summer after graduation before college, and it is remembered as a great place to work, for great people, with the Christmas parties thrown in.

And it was full of mothers. Ask around and you will hear it over and over: my mom worked there, my mom waitressed there in the late sixties, my mom worked there in the seventies. Grandmothers too. You might stop in to visit your grandma while she worked, or go up to see your mom and get something cold to drink before the two of you walked home. Tony's wife, Bonnie, was the hostess for a while. Vince's son Nick ran the kitchen. As long as the ladies did not catch you, you could get a pack of cigarettes out of the machine.

## Up the stairs

The V&A was also where life got marked. Wedding rehearsal dinners and wedding receptions, family parties, work Christmas parties. Teams ate their pre-game meals there, football before home games, basketball before games at East Noble. And if you were in town for the blizzard of '78 and walked uptown every day, the V&A had hot chocolate, and coffee and hot chocolate was about all they had left.

Then there was upstairs. The Eagles were up a flight of narrow stairs, right above the restaurant or next door, depending on who you ask, and nobody fully agrees. Those stairs were murder to carry a Fender amp up, and square dances got called up there. Later there was a disco, the word right there on the side of the awning, and Don Moore spun the records. You went for the dancing on weekends, and you might have met your wife there. Plenty of people do not remember a disco at all.

## The Jade Buddha

The place became the Landmark for a while, and then it became something else entirely: the Jade Buddha, a Chinese restaurant, and it became a staple. It had the best egg rolls ever. It had the Jade Buddha steak, steaks with Chinese vegetables, and when that was not in the budget, it was always the chicken sum gum. The burger, it must be admitted, tasted a little strange. You might have eaten there alone every Saturday for years, your favorite place for a quiet meal, and they always remembered you and what you wanted, and after they closed you taught yourself to make egg drop soup.

## After

It is gone from Main Street now, and so is the Main Street that went with it, the one that was so alive back in those days. Vince is not coming out of the kitchen with his cigar to ask how everything was. It was good, Vince. It was all so good. Too bad you cannot go back and do some of the good stuff again.
