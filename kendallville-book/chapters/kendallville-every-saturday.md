# Every Saturday
*The Strand Theatre and the Hi-Vue Drive-In*

It was for kids first. Kids with a handful of pennies, kids on bikes, kids who went to the show just about every Saturday. You could be seven years old, standing in line on Main Street with your brother and no parent anywhere in sight. Life was simple. In the fifties the bikes lined up along the curb on a Saturday afternoon, what seemed like hundreds of them, all the way down to the Palace of Sweets, and inside the Strand it was the Three Stooges, Ma and Pa Kettle, Francis the Talking Mule and then a double feature. Twelve cents, if you could talk your grandpa out of a few more pennies. Life was great for a kid in those days.

Later it was a quarter to get in, thirty-five cents if it was a special picture. Popcorn was a dime and candy was a nickel, and you can still see yourself at that candy counter waiting on your Milk Duds.

## The booth out front

You bought your ticket outside, at the little booth on the sidewalk, and when it got really cold they sold them from inside instead. At twelve you paid the adult price, and that was the rule. If you were a tall kid, the cashier would never believe you were still eleven, and you might have ended up carrying a miniature copy of your birth certificate just to prove it.

Nobody agrees on what the lobby looked like. Some see it bright. Others will tell you it was darker, much darker, by the seventies, more burgundy than anything. There were little steps up to the drinking fountain, and you may remember the rabbits on the ends of them.

Go around the corner to the left and there were the stairs to the balcony.

## Mr. Point

The balcony was the prize. It was a treat to go to the Strand at all, and even better if you got to sit up there. But the balcony was also where Cleon Point did his patrolling. He ran the Strand, and Mr. Point made sure everyone behaved. If you were fidgeting up there during the movie, he could sneak up there and grab your arm. One warning was all you got. Two, and you were watching the rest of it from the ground floor.

And yet. He wore a hat a lot. He always looked sharp. He acted rough and tough, and he was the sweetest man. He ran the Strand like a true gentleman. You could sit through the same Godzilla picture over and over on a Saturday and he never threw you out.

They say the Strand is haunted now.

## The line down the block

Some movies were events. For Jaws in 1975 the line went out the door and down past Klinkenberg's and around the corner. It was the same for Smokey and the Bandit, and the Star Wars line took forever. Your first movie at the adult price might have been The Empire Strikes Back. After Halloween you did not want to walk down the alley beside the theater.

There were Kraft Christmas parties at the Strand, and after the movie Santa was in the lobby handing out presents to the Kraft employees' children. After the Thanksgiving Day Parade you could go in to see Santa there too and come out with a sack full of candy and fruit.

And when the picture was over you walked to the Palace of Sweets, or to Central Drugs for a burger basket. At the Palace a burger, fries and a shake came to ninety-seven cents.

## The first job

For a lot of Kendallville teenagers the Strand was also the first job. Candy counter, ticket booth, usher, janitor, and changing the letters on the marquee after the movie. The pay was thirty-five cents an hour in 1963, and $1.90 by the middle seventies. You might work the Hi-Vue and the Auburn-Garrett too. The best part of the job was seeing all the new movies for free, up in the balcony. There was an upstairs room full of old posters that would be worth a fortune today.

In 1980 the Strand was split into two theaters.

## The Hi-Vue

Out on State Road 3 was the Hi-Vue Drive-In. The Hi-Vue was where you went with your mom and dad and rode in the back seat, in your pajamas, with pillows and blankets. Your mother made a bed back there. There was a pitcher of Kool-Aid and your own popcorn, or a stop first at the popcorn stand uptown.

You might have lain on the hood of the car with a big box of popcorn and woken up the next morning in your own bed, having missed most of the movie. It was a fine place to catch up on your sleep. If it rained the night you saw Old Yeller, you might have believed for years that the sad scene was shot in the rain.

Then you got older and the back seat meant something else. It was everyone out of the cars and talking to people in other cars. It was a tap on the window from whoever was walking the lot. It was where people met the one they married. Jailhouse Rock, Fantastic Voyage, The Love Bug, Star Wars, An Officer and a Gentleman: the Hi-Vue showed them all.

It is where Snyder's is now.

The Strand is still on Main Street, still showing movies. But the booth out front is gone, and the twelve-cent Saturday, and the man in the hat who kept order in the balcony. It seemed so magical back then. It was. You were there for it.
