# After School and After the Bars
*Chronister's counter, the Gingerbread House, the Chuck Wagon and the Publix Corners Cafe*

Before the drive-thrus took over, Kendallville kept its own clock of places to sit down and be fed. There was a place for the kid with fry money after school, a place for Saturday supper if you had been good, a place for Sunday breakfast after church, and a place that was still open, lights on and coffee hot, when the bars let out. None of them was fancy. All of them knew you.

## The counter in the back

You walked in past the pharmacy and the Hallmark cards, and the restaurant was the back half of the store. For most of the town's memory it was Chronister's, downtown; to older ears it was the Central Drug Store, the same counter under an earlier name. You went after school. You went on Saturdays. You went every day after school, if you could get away with it, and ordered the same thing every time.

The thing was a green river. Lime-green, sweet, a soft drink you had to explain to anybody from somewhere else: very sweet, sugary green goodness. You can still quote the price, twenty-one cents. If green rivers weren't your drink, the fountain made cherry cokes, vanilla cokes, chocolate cokes, lemon cokes, and if you worked behind it you learned to make a Suicide, a shot of every syrup in one glass. There was always a small, stubborn minority who never liked green rivers at all, and they will still tell you so.

With it came a cheeseburger basket and fries. The fries were the best ever, or crispy, or greasy, and all three are meant as praise. The ketchup was either the best in town or watered down, depending on who you ask. The club sandwich was a first for more than a few kids, and the house sandwich was the Jolly Roger: toasted ham and cheese with coleslaw right on it, a combination you tried to make at home years later and never quite got right. You could send yourself next door from the grown-up restaurant and page through the magazine rack for Mad while you worked on a chocolate coke. You bought comic books there. Your grandfather bought you candy cigarettes.

Chronister's left downtown around 1990 and closed for good in 2009. There is no Chronister's now, and no counter.

## One corner, three names

Out on Dowling at Park Avenue, back when Dowling still carried US 6 through town, there was a building with gables on it. The oldest name for it is the Three Gables: a gas station, a bay for working on cars, and a small restaurant. When it turned into the Gingerbread House, a dad could joke that he was eating over the grease pit.

The Gingerbread House was Henny Penny chicken. Broasted, the first broasted chicken around, and it came in a red box with chickens all over it. Beside it went the hobo potatoes, or the broasted potatoes, or the potato wedges, depending on what your family called them, and everyone who ate them calls them the best. There were pies, and gingerbread, and a chef salad big enough to be remembered for fifty years. It was a family dinner place, a grandparents place, a rehearsal-dinner place. It was a treat. It was a special Saturday night meal if you were good kids.

Then it became the Chuck Wagon, and the corner learned to stay up late.

Chuck and Stella Streb ran it, and nobody who went there says their names apart. Stella had an awesome smile. She was a hoot, the Chuck Wagon's own Flo from Mel's Diner. The biscuits and gravy were the best in town, the fried taters came with onions, and breakfast was served at an hour when nothing else in Kendallville was serving anything.

That was the point. When the bars closed, the Chuck Wagon filled. One in the morning, three in the morning, after the Moose, after the Parlor, after the J&J. Chuck would tell you plainly that he catered to the drinkers, and he enjoyed doing it. On a big Saturday night every booth was taken, and you might carry your lawn chair in from the car just to have somewhere to sit. The coffee pots floated from table to table. If you got up to fill your cup, you poured for the room until the pot was empty. You might slide into the last open booth and find it backed right up against a booth full of police, eating their own breakfast. How Chuck and Stella put up with that crowd is still a mystery, and the crowd loved them dearly for it.

In daylight it belonged to other people. Grandmothers walked their grandchildren there for lunch. You went on Friday for lunch with your mom, or on Saturday morning to see your aunt and uncle behind the counter. Plenty of kids from that side of town had their first job there, waiting tables for Chuck and Stella.

## Out at the corner of 6 and 3

The Publix Corners Cafe sat at the junction of US 6 and State Road 3, with a service station and a car dealership along the same stretch of building. It was Gardner's, and it was the place to go for special occasions: a birthday supper, a wedding reception, breakfast after church with your grandparents on Sunday.

You ordered liver and onions, and they were delicious. After supper you got a scoop of ice cream in a little metal dish. The pies were made right there, by a baker in her eighties who came in every day at 6:30. The salad bar was made there too, and so were the warm pecan rolls. So good, and so bad for you. If your father was Rotary, Tuesday nights he ate at the cafe, and you stayed home with a TV dinner and thought that was the treat. There was a man who drove over from Angola for church and ate there on Sundays, and he parked by the window so he could wave at his poodle waiting in the car.

## Still under the roof

The corner at Dowling and Park went on to be beauty shops and offices and, in time, a computer business. The peaks of the old gables are still under the roof, and somewhere above a drop ceiling hangs the original garage door, because nobody could ever get it out.

You can still buy green river in a bottle. It is not as good. You knew it wouldn't be.
