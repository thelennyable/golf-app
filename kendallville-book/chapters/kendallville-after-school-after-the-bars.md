# After School and After the Bars
*Chronister's counter, the Gingerbread House, the Chuck Wagon and the Publix Corners Cafe*

Kendallville used to keep its own clock of places to sit down and be fed. There was a place for fries and a cherry coke after school, a place for Saturday supper if you had been good, a place for breakfast after church, and a place that still had the coffee on when the bars let out.

## The counter in the back

The pharmacy and the Hallmark shop were the front half of the store, and the restaurant was the back half. For most of the town's memory it was Chronister's, downtown; to older ears it was the Central Drug Store, the same counter under an earlier name. You went after school. You went on Saturdays. You went every day after school, if you could get away with it.

The thing was a green river. Lime-green, sweet, a soft drink you still have to explain to people: very sweet, sugary green goodness. You can still quote the price, twenty-one cents. If green rivers weren't your drink, the fountain made cherry cokes, vanilla cokes, chocolate cokes, lemon cokes, and if you worked behind it you learned to make a Suicide, a shot of every syrup in one glass. There was always a small, stubborn minority who never liked green rivers at all, and they will still tell you so.

With it came a cheeseburger basket and fries. The fries were the best ever, or crispy, or greasy, and all three are meant as praise. The ketchup was either the best in town or watered down, depending on who you ask. The club sandwich there could be the first one you ever ate, and the house sandwich was the Jolly Roger: toasted ham and cheese with coleslaw right on it, a combination you tried to make at home years later and never quite got right. If you got on your parents' nerves at the Nobleman next door, they might send you over, and you paged through the magazine rack for Mad while you worked through a chocolate coke. You bought comic books there. Your grandfather bought you candy cigarettes.

Chronister's closed for good in 2009. There is no Chronister's now, and no counter.

## One corner, three names

Out on Dowling at Park Avenue, back when Dowling still carried US 6 through town, there was a building with gables on it. The oldest name for it is the Three Gables: a gas station, a bay for working on cars, and a small restaurant.

The Gingerbread House was Henny Penny chicken. Broasted, and it came in a red box with chickens all over it. Beside it went the hobo potatoes, or the broasted potatoes, or the potato wedges, depending on what your family called them, and they were the best. There were pies, and gingerbread, and huge chef salads. It was a family dinner place, a grandparents place. It was a treat. It was a special Saturday night meal if you were good kids.

Then it became the Chuck Wagon, and the corner learned to stay up late.

Chuck and Stella Streb ran it, and their names come as a pair, always: Chuck and Stella. Stella had an awesome smile. She was a hoot, the Chuck Wagon's own Flo from Mel's Diner. The biscuits and gravy were the best in town, the fried taters came with onions, and you could go in and eat breakfast at three in the morning.

That was the point. When the bars closed, the Chuck Wagon filled. One in the morning, three in the morning, after the Moose, after the Parlor, after the J&J. Chuck would tell you plainly that he catered to the drinkers. On a Saturday night it could get so busy that you carried your own lawn chair in just to have somewhere to sit. The coffee pots floated from table to table. If you got up to fill your cup, you poured for the room until the pot was empty. You might slide into the last open booth and find it backed right up against a booth full of police, eating their own breakfast. How Chuck and Stella put up with that crowd is still a mystery, and the crowd loved them both dearly.

In daylight it belonged to other people. You went early in the morning with your grandma. You went on Friday for lunch with your mom. Plenty of kids had their first job there, waiting tables for Chuck and Stella.

## Out at the corner of 6 and 3

The Publix Corners Cafe sat at the junction of US 6 and State Road 3, with a service station and a car dealership along the same stretch of building. It was the place to go for special occasions: a birthday supper, a wedding reception, breakfast after church with your grandparents on Sunday.

You ordered liver and onions, and they were delicious. After supper you got a scoop of ice cream in a little metal dish. The pies were made right there, by a baker in her eighties who came in every day at 6:30. The salad bar was made there too, and so were the warm pecan rolls. So good, and so bad for you. If your father was Rotary, Tuesday nights he ate at the cafe, and you stayed home with a TV dinner and thought that was the treat.

## Still under the roof

The building at Dowling and Park has been beauty shops and a lawyer's office, and these days a computer business. The peaks of the old gables are still under the roof, and the original garage door from the Three Gables is still up there too, inside a drop ceiling.

You can still buy green river in a bottle. It is not as good. You knew it wouldn't be.
