# Those Were the Days
*Cruising Main Street*

This is for anybody who ever had a car, or a friend with one, and a weekend night with nowhere in particular to be. It is for the kid who could not legally drive yet and went along anyway. It is for the ones who came over from Central Noble, from Garrett and Prairie Heights, from DeKalb and Lakeland, from Auburn once the square got boring, because as far as you were concerned Albion was too slow. Cruising Kendallville was how you socialized. It was the way to see and be seen.

Nobody could even agree what to call it. In the sixties it was just driving around. Then it was chopping town, which sounded a little silly if you had grown up calling it driving around. Say "cruising" in the wrong company today and you will be put right: we didn't cruise, we chopped town, get it right. Call it both if you like; it was the same Main Street either way, your very own American Graffiti, except there was no Wolfman Jack. There was whoever was the weekend DJ on WMEE.

## The loop

Everybody's loop was a little different, depending on the decade. Down Main, left on 6, then up and around the Redwood or the DQ, depending on whether there had been a ballgame. Or north on Main from the Sinclair station to the Dairy Queen, and repeat. Or down Main to the DQ and back to Diamond, out to the lake, and start over. Burger Chef was a turnaround too. Before that, the older kids went down Main, left onto Diamond, left onto Oak, left onto Rush and right back onto Main.

It was not fast. Main Street went at a snail's pace. Sometimes it took thirty or forty-five minutes to get from one end to the other, or longer if there was a train going through. Late in the eighties it could take an hour one way. Your dad could not work out how anybody racked up fifty miles cruising Kendallville, and you could not have explained it to him.

Gas was under a dollar a gallon, and you chipped in your coins for it. You wish now you had half the gas you wasted. And if you didn't want to waste any, you could park at the corner of 6 and Main and just watch all night, or sit at the gas station across from the police station in the evening and watch the cars go by. In the eighties you could still pull into a parking lot, watch the traffic gather and hang out. By the nineties you had maybe ten or fifteen minutes before the police came down the road and everybody scattered.

## The nights

The radio was turned up. The music was bumping. You played CB tag. You started the night in one car with some friends and ended it in another car with other friends. You went to meet girls, to find out where the parties were, to meet girls again, and to show off your car. And sometimes it worked better than that: marriages started on Main Street, with a boy cruising in from Prairie Heights, or on a night you ditched the car and walked the street instead.

There were water guns, in the middle eighties and later, meant for friends. At least once a squirt gun found the open window of a grown-up who had only gone out to Rogers for groceries. The cops would hassle you for loud music, or for parking where you shouldn't, but for the most part they let you alone.

## The cars

That was why you played with cars; it was all you had. You could spend the whole day cleaning the car and then head to town. Who could afford a camera when all your money went into your ride?

So the roll call is long, and a lot of it comes with the same wish: that you still had it. A '67 Chevelle Malibu. Camaros, a Vette, a Trans Am, a '77 gold Nova, an '86 Camaro with t-tops. A '66 GTO, a '67 GTO convertible, a red '65 GTO with a 389 and three deuces. A '71 'Cuda 340 six-pack, a '69 Formula S Barracuda with the 383 and a four-speed, a blue '73 440 Barracuda with white stripes, a green '70 'Cuda. A '68 Torino convertible, a '66 Grand Prix, a '69 Roadrunner, a '68 Olds 442, a red '69 AMX, a '69 Chevelle SS 396, a '71 Firebird. A blue '66 Chevelle called Mr. Evil, cruising past the Wake Up station.

And not all of it was muscle. A '76 Cadillac Coupe de Ville. A Gremlin that, whatever else it was, got you there. A Jeep. A purple dune buggy. A 1970 Impala, purple over a black top, with a crucifix superglued to the dashboard. A station wagon that used more oil than gas. A Monte Carlo SS, a Lincoln, an American Rambler.

## No pictures

Ask for photographs of it now and mostly you will be told there aren't any. There were no cell phones, not even pagers. Film cost money. Who carried a camera to chop town? And plenty will tell you that this was for the best: no cameras meant no proof, and nobody wanted any evidence floating around town. When you are in it, it's just another night. It is years later that the pictures become magic, and by then the only pictures are in your head.

Every decade is sure it was the best one. The fifties had better music and better cars. You should have been here for the sixties. The seventies say nobody else can hold a candle to them. The eighties say that is when it was real fun. The nineties cruised too, and if you came up earlier, you may blame the nineties for killing it.

## The barricades

Not everyone loved it. The people who lived on Main Street grumbled. If you just needed gas or groceries, it could take thirty minutes to get through town, and if you were the one stuck in it, you were glad when chopping stopped. The city started blocking off roads. Barricades went up, by the tracks on Main Street. Nobody quite agrees when it ended, somewhere around the late eighties or the early nineties, and some took it to Goshen instead.

Years later, one night, people got together to remember cruising down Main Street, starting at eight. The traffic was back, and the revving engines and the burnouts and the bass. There were high school friends, and older generations and younger ones, lots of honking and waving, even Albion cops driving through and waving and honking, and an EMS driver who honked and flashed the lights. And they were doing it again next Friday.

But the cars are sold, the gas is not under a dollar, and the barricades did their work. What is left is what you carried home: you, your ride, and whatever the night brought. Those were the days. You wish you still had those kinds of days.
