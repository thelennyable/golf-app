# Dad Took Me
*The barbershops of Kendallville*

Before you were old enough to pick a barber, the choice was made for you. Ask anybody who grew up a boy in Kendallville where they got their hair cut, and a lot of the answers start the same way. Dad took me. Dad took me to Ralph's. Dad took me to Pete and Gabby. Dad took me to Walt Martz. Ask who got their hair cut at Carl's and the answers come back like a roll call of fathers and grandfathers: my dad, my grandpa, my dad and brothers, my daddy did.

And before any of that, there was the kitchen. A chair set in the middle of the floor, and your mother, or your dad with the clippers going zip zip. You might have had a towel around your neck. You might have sat under the plastic cover in a house with no air conditioning, the itchy hair stuck to your sweaty neck, and the smell of the electric motor. You might have been the kid whose bangs got a strip of tape across them as a guide. The barbershop was a promotion.

Kendallville had a lot of barbers, and everybody had theirs. Pete and Gabby. Red's. Al's. Martz. Smitty's. Shaneline's, which became Carl's. Bob Frick. You could go your whole childhood in one chair, or you could go to Bob, Al, Gabby, and Red once, and play the field.

Pete and Gabby were a pair, and they come out in one breath. Where Pete and Gabby's shop actually stood depends on who you ask and which years they mean: Mitchell Street, or Williams Street, somewhere just off Main. Nobody quite agrees. Pete and Gabby were nice guys. You could spend a Saturday morning there with your dad and your brother, watching cartoons on the TV above the door. There was the padded board laid across the armrests for the little guys, and the day you could sit in the chair without it felt close to growing up. Somehow you always ended up with the pineapple cut. If you knew what you wanted, you asked for it plain: butch, with a little left in front.

Gabby also cut hair in the basement of the Kendall Hotel, and so did Warren Sower.

Red's was downstairs too, the basement on Main Street under Kirkwood's, the one with the stairs going down next to the popcorn stand.

Walt Martz cut hair on South Main. Sit in his chair and you had the smell of the tonic and the leather strops for sharpening the blade, and if you behaved, a sucker at the end.

Then there was Wayne Street, by Jim's Pizza, where Ralph Shaneline cut hair before Carl Wagner took over the shop. Ralph's was the 1950s and 1960s for a lot of Kendallville men. You could sit and listen to a whole discussion of what size rims were on whose car and who had the lowest license plate number.

Carl's came after, same street, same shop, and for a long time it was the place your dad and grandpa went. It was not only a place for a haircut. You could buy fishing bait there, and out front stood the live bait vending machine. If you were a kid with buckets of night crawlers after a rain, Carl would buy them from you for a couple of cents apiece. And Carl talked. Your dad could go in for a haircut and come out two hours later. Not every cut was a masterpiece. There are grown adults in this town still describing their worst haircut ever, and laughing about it. Never get a haircut where they sell fish bait.

Bob Frick is the name that comes back more than any other. For a lot of Kendallville, Bob was the man, the best, first in his shop behind the old Dairy Queen and later downtown on South Main at Family Shears. You could wait your turn with his vast collection of Mad magazines. You could go to Bob from high school on, for twenty years or forty, until he retired.

There were years, of course, when the haircuts got scarce. It was the sixties, and later the seventies, and a lot of Kendallville simply did not get many haircuts.

What you remember is not really the cut. It is going with your dad, or your grandpa, the pineapple cut, the talk about rims and license plates, the board across the armrests. No wonder we all looked alike.

Those barbers are gone from their chairs now. Gabby and Al are names you find in obituaries. Bob hung up his clippers. Carl's shop on Wayne Street stopped cutting hair. And if you sat on one of those boards, it may not matter much now which chair you would choose, because in the final analysis, there is not much hair left to cut.
