# A Pop and a Candy Bar
*The filling stations*

Before you pumped your own gas and walked inside to pay, there was the station, and a kid on a bicycle knew exactly what it was for. You rode up, you checked the air in your tires, you bought a pop for a dime and a candy bar, and if you stayed long enough you got an endless amount of wisdom, or an endless amount of something else, from the hard-working man who owned the place. Your father bought his gas there. You bought your pop there. The same man sold both.

They had names, and the names were the stations. Ben Amerman's. The Baker Brothers Texaco. John Riemke's. Benny's. Mr. Stonebraker's, Mr. Antonides's. Mister, because if you called one of them by his first name, your father might have kicked your rear. Your father could use their first names. You could not.

## Ben's

Ben's station sat up at Diamond and Main, the northeast corner, most people will tell you. It was the first stop on a paper route: you folded your News-Sun there before you set out, and bought a Mallow Cup for energy. When the route passed from one boy to the next, the stop at Ben's passed with it. It was a place for pop and chips after school. Ask what the pop was like and you will hear the same answer more than once: cold. The coldest pop in town.

If your dad got gas at Ben's on a Friday, there might be a piece of candy in it for you. For a while there was dinosaur soap, bars of soap shaped like dinosaurs, handed out as a promotion, and you have not forgotten it. There were card games there, a lot of them.

Ben did the work on a lot of Kendallville cars: oil changes, small repairs. He could fix anything. He had a good reputation, and the word that comes up about him, over and over, is nice. A nice guy, a super nice guy, a really nice man, a good guy.

And then there was the yard. Out at the corner of Leighty and Angling roads, Ben had a salvage yard, and if you were a Kendallville kid who kept an old car alive on no money, you went there. Used parts, alternators and starters for fifteen dollars, tires for five dollars apiece, parts for a '61 Buick LeSabre, parts for a race car. You could walk the field just looking at the cars. The floors in the barn creaked so badly you wondered if it was going to come down around you. Ben kept a collection too, a museum of cars and toys and collectibles, and when it finally went to auction, the barn was still full.

## The Texaco on North Street

Out on US 6, which the town called North Street, at the corner of Riley, the old Cemetery Road, stood the Baker Brothers Texaco. Paul and Harvey Baker owned it. Harvey "Brack" Baker drove stock cars, and you could stand at the station and watch him fine-tuning the car before the weekly races at the fairgrounds. The car was number 67, the same as the station's telephone number, which was 67. It was, as it is remembered, kind of a bubblegum pink.

A high school boy could get a job there. You pumped a lot of gas and washed a lot of windshields, changed oil and fixed tires, and when you needed to call a girl, there was a pay phone inside. A neighborhood child could walk up for goodies, pop and candy, and the man running the place was nice to you.

The corner changed hands and changed flags. At one time or another it was Kennie Haynes's station, and Kennie had Edsels, two or three of them, and one sat on the side where you could look it over. There was a pinball machine. At some point the sign read 66. Behind the station was Kelsey's, and the corner and the root beer stand run together in the memory so completely that you may only remember the root beer stand being there at all.

A bank sits on that corner now.

## Riemke's, on one side of 6 or the other

John Riemke came to Kendallville to run the Shell station on US 6 at State Street, and for a while he ran the Gulf station nearly across the street as well, until he gave up the Shell and kept the Gulf, because what he loved was the tire business. He hired a lot of high schoolers. The Gulf sat next to Chuck's Tavern. If you drove north on State Street across 6, you drove right into it. Then John Riemke became the mayor, and stayed the mayor for twenty years.

Ask where his station was and you will start an argument. North side of 6. No, south side, where the Walgreens is. He had both, it was both. There was a Shell, there was a Gulf, and the Medical Arts building sat behind one of them, and everyone has the map in their head, and the maps do not agree.

The Shell corner is the Walgreens parking lot now. Where the Gulf stood, the last name on the sign was Certified, and after that came a real estate office. Tell a lifelong Kendallville resident that there was once a gas station on that corner, and you may be told you are crazy.

## Benny's

Benny ran a City Service station on North Main at the corner of Rush, before he ever went on the police department. On a Saturday morning you could hang around and watch them wash cars and drink a pop. The News-Sentinel dropped its papers there for the carriers. Benny walked to the station every day, and when he joined the police department, he walked the couple of blocks to that job too.

Kendallville knew him as the station man, as a school crossing guard, and as a police officer. If you were sixteen and he was the first cop ever to pull you over, you might have driven away without a ticket. He was always so great to the kids. He really did care.

## What was lost

What those men sold was gas, and oil, and tires. What they gave away was harder to price. You could walk in with a car you were keeping alive on spare parts and ask the owner about your oil pump, and he would give you his advice. Now you pump your own gas and walk inside to pay, and try asking the attendant about an oil pump.

That kind of service is long gone. So is the kid on the bicycle with a dime for a cold pop. The corners are still there, most of them. A drugstore on one, a bank on another, a real estate office where the Gulf used to be. Nobody washes your windshield.
