# Smelled Like Money
*The Kendallville foundries*

There was a kind of Kendallville household where the alarm went off at four in the morning, or half past three, and the man of the house was out the door before anyone else had turned over. He did not take vacations. He did not call in sick, because there was a roof to keep over the kids and they had to eat, and that was the whole of the argument. He came home in the afternoon black from collar to hairline, sometimes black everywhere but two pale circles where his safety glasses had been. His father had very likely done the same job before him. His brothers were probably on the next shift. A good share of those families had come up from the coal country of Kentucky because this was where the work was, and the work was iron.

Kendallville was a foundry town, and two names mattered most. On West Ohio Street, straight across from Kraft, stood Newnam Foundry, which changed owners and signs more than once and ended its life as Dalton, though half the town never stopped calling it Newnam's, or Newman's, or simply the foundry. Out along U.S. 6 stood the Kendallville Foundry, a different plant altogether, tied for years to Flint & Walling and pouring the castings the pump works needed. Around those two, in the memory of anyone who worked iron, sit the other names: Lane's, North End, the smaller shops. Between them they fed a great many families in this corner of Indiana.

Inside it was heat and noise and sand. The cupola was the heart of it, a furnace that had to be lit and tended and fed until the iron ran, and on a bad morning it could blow out the ductwork and turn the whole building black with dust. Shovel coal into that thing and your glasses could nearly melt to your face. Pouring iron was the roughest, hottest job in the place, and it could still be the one you loved. Behind the pour lay a whole geography of work that families at home learned by name without ever seeing it: the core room, the grinding room, the melt shop, the sand room, the mold line, the hot line, the shaker, the shipping dock. A child could grow up knowing that his father mixed the sand for the molds, or shifted weights, or ran the cupola, the way other children knew their fathers sold insurance.

If your father could sneak you in at night to watch a pour, you never forgot it. The iron came out the color of the sun, and for a few minutes the dirtiest place in town was the most impressive thing you had ever seen.

The foundry did not keep to its own fence. Before the scrubbers went on the stack, you could drive past at night and see the whole works lit up around the open furnace. If flame came up out of the chimney while you were bringing a date home and the date asked what that was, you could say all hell was breaking loose, and you would not be far wrong. A day or two after a snowfall, the snow on that side of town went black. And there was the smell, which nobody can describe and nobody has forgotten. It hung over the south side and got into the houses along Main Street and into the men themselves. Some hated it. Others miss it to this day. It got into your blood. Catching it on the wind meant you were home. To a foundry man it smelled like money.

You rode along with your mother to bring your father his lunch, or his supper if he was on second shift, and waited by the guard shack and the turning gate for him to come out. If you were lucky he came back with a Coke in a paper cup, and it had the best ice you have ever had in your life. Some days the lunch run went by way of Blue's on South Main for fresh sandwiches. If you lived close enough, you could sit on the back yard swing in the late afternoon and watch for the shape of your father, or your grandfather, walking home across the field and over the hill.

It was not only men. Women worked the lunch rooms, where the black dust found you too. Women kept the office for decades, and women worked out on the floor as well: the core room, the mold line, the metal lab. You could meet your husband there. Whole branches of a family tree can be traced through the payroll: a grandfather in the core room, a father on the pour, uncles in grinding and maintenance, a daughter in the office, a son-in-law driving truck. It was hard to find anyone around Kendallville who did not have family at Newnam's, and the foundry men were one another's family as much as their own.

For the town's college boys it was a summer job, often third shift, good money, and the single best reason ever invented to go back to school in the fall. One day of grinding castings or working the hot line made a semester of lectures look restful.

Nobody pretends it was clean. The work could put metal in your eyes and take your hearing, and a hot cinder down the top of a boot left burns that took a long time to heal. The harder argument is about the lungs. Across town you will hear it in the same breath as the pride: emphysema, COPD, lung cancer, men who did not live to collect the pension they had spent their lives earning. You will hear the other side just as firmly. You can live three blocks from the plant, work there thirty-three years, come out healthy, and refuse to hear a word against the place that put food on the table and the kids through school. Both are true in Kendallville, and nobody has settled it.

What the men kept were small pieces of iron. Indian head bookends. A little cast iron man pouring from a hand ladle. A frog, a trivet, a round hot plate. And when the end came, a plaque from the last pour, which sits now on living room shelves and fireplace mantels, next to the photographs.

The Dalton foundry on Ohio Street closed in 2009. The Kendallville Foundry out on U.S. 6 had gone quiet long before. Both were torn down, and if you drive past where either one stood, you will find yourself slowing the car without meaning to.

Kendallville learned what it had been when the smell was gone. A foundry town is a town where you know what your father does because you can see it on him when he walks in the door. If your family had worked there three generations, you might have been the fourth. Now there is the plaque on the shelf, and a town that still, on certain summer nights, half expects the sky to go orange over the south side.
