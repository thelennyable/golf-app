# Skate Night
*The Kendallville Youth Center*

For a long run of years, the kids of Kendallville had a building of their own. You stopped in on the walk home from school and hung out for a while. You went after school to play bumper pool in the game room. You played there almost every day. If you were small enough, you were hauled in there for preschool. It was a great, safe place to be with your friends, and for a few hours at a time it was a place to have fun and never worry about anything. It was the Youth Center, or just Y C.

Ask what it was for and the answer is roller skating, roller skating, roller skating. The skates were strap-ons, or the kind that clamped to the sole of your shoe and tightened with a key. You learned by holding on to another kid's shirttail and getting dragged around on your skates until you could do it yourself. Your fourth-grade year, you went skating. In one stretch of years skate night was fifty cents on a Wednesday, and they always played "Another One Bites the Dust." You could not wait for skate night.

When you got too old simply to skate, you worked it. At sign-up the jobs went first come, first served: skate rental, skate helper, the snack bar, or DJ, if you got your name down in time. It could be your first job. You could skate there as a kid and come back to work skate night.

Then there were the dances. After the football games and the basketball games, in the late fifties and the sixties, everybody went to the Youth Center. Home games were the best. Some of the best of times were right here in River City. There were live bands, local rock bands, a band that practiced there. There were record parties too. At the junior high dances the boys could line up along one wall and leave the girls to dance with each other, and the boys did not know what they were missing. There were dances where that was not the case. Then the slow dances. Innocence, all of it. Junior High Night was always fun. Your mom and dad might come along to chaperone.

There were nights that did not end. Junior high all-night parties. Overnight skate parties. All-night slumber parties where you slept on that old, cold floor. A teen council, working with Lois, who ran the activities, lined up the bands, the hayrides and the overnights. Lois worked hard with the kids. She was a peach, a fantastic lady, something else, and a little bit scary. Upstairs she taught crafts, and you could come home with a furry worm creeper and keep it for a long time. Upstairs was its own country: the arts and crafts, the drama club, the classrooms.

The building held more than a single evening could use. Gymnastics classes. Ballet, dance lessons, cooking classes, drama club in the late sixties, judo, aerobics and exercise classes, arts and crafts, preschool. It was a great way to spend a day. A slot car track, and those slot cars were wonderful. A BB gun shooting contest. Boys' night and girls' night. And boxing. Brothers boxed there, and you could go watch your brother box, and you could learn there, the first and last time you ever boxed, that getting hit on the nose brings tears to your eyes. At closing time an older man sent everybody home with the same line: okey dokey, no more cokey.

It is still there. The park department keeps its offices in it now. People walk indoors there at set hours. It has held reunions and weddings, wedding receptions and Christmas parties, with a big stage and lots of room to play and, of course, roller skating. You went there, your kids went there, your grandkids went there. On Monday nights it is still skate night for the kids, and somewhere on that floor is the fourth grader you were.

The bands after the home games. The boys along the wall. The slot cars. The skates that needed a key. The overnight on the cold floor with your friends. Best days, now long gone. The floor is still there. It is only the kids who are not kids anymore.
