# I Was Born There
*McCray Memorial Hospital*

Ask Kendallville where it started out and the answer is already on its way before you finish the question. I was born there. Born there in '58, in '65, in '71, in '79, in '91. Sometimes the answer is nothing but a date, as if the date explains itself: 9/21/1944. 1-20-1965. 1942. Then comes the rest of the family. My brother and sister. My mother, too. All three of my kids were born there. All four of mine. All five. Me and my kids and my grandson. A mother, her son and his daughter, three generations started in the same building. For most of the twentieth century, if you were from Kendallville, you came into the world at the hospital by the lake, and so did most of your classmates.

It sat on Bixler Lake, with the park beside it. It had been Lakeside Hospital first, and the McCray name came to it later, after the McCray family gave so much to it. If you were born in the forties, you may remember tall white pillars at the front entrance and a smaller building, the big steps, the circle drive. It grew over the years, additions and remodels, the old part standing behind the newer front, until it is hard to say which building anyone means by the old McCray. The old brick one. The old old building. The one to the left of the update. There were enclosed porch rooms on the lake side called solariums, with beds for patients getting better, looking out over Bixler Lake.

For a while the maternity ward was on the second floor, behind the triple windows. If you had your baby there in the late fifties, it may have been in a wing overlooking the park hill and the lake, sharing the ward with eight or so other new mothers, and for three days you all talked about how to raise babies, and what scared you about it, and you laughed. In labor, you might walk the small OB department. The babies were brought into the world by doctors whose names still come back easily, Dr. Hepner and Dr. Chandler and Dr. Greenlee, and Dr. Hepner's name still comes with a thank-you.

Kids weren't allowed in the rooms back then. If your father was upstairs, you stood outside and looked up at a second-floor window until he waved. If a baby brother had just arrived, you might be taken up on the roof to see him through the window. Or, once he was well enough, they brought your dad down to the lobby in a wheelchair, because you were too young to go upstairs.

And plenty of Kendallville left something there. Tonsils, mostly. You left your tonsils there, and maybe later your appendix. If you were six, it was where you were introduced to popsicles. If you came through the front door with your Brownie troop, what you remember is the strong smell of ether when you walked in.

Then there were the candy stripers. You couldn't wait until you were old enough to be one. Born there, candy striper there, then back again to have your own children there: that is a whole life in one building, and more than a few Kendallville girls lived it. You might wash microscope slides and ogle the cute interns, or clean the blood draw tubes in the lab, long before the tubes were disposable, or help out in X-ray when there were no patients and on the floor when there were. And you learned to spell it carefully, because one letter makes candy striper into something else entirely, it slips in again and again, and there is always a joke waiting for it.

It was a small hospital, and a great many Kendallville mothers worked in it: nurses and nurse's aides, the switchboard, the gift shop, the maternity floor. The people who worked there talk about it the way you talk about family. Which meant a lot of Kendallville kids grew up in that building too. You walked over from St. John Lutheran school and sat in the south lobby waiting for your mother to get off work, or you played in the park next door while she worked. When school was delayed or cancelled, you went to work with her. Home sick, you might sleep all day in an ER bed. Home away from home.

There was a snack bar and a little gift shop with a grill in it, and the patients loved that gift shop. If you had the News-Sun route, you dropped the papers off there and bought a 3 Musketeers bar on your way out. For a lot of Kendallville teenagers the hospital was the first job: the snack bar in high school, the dish room, the cafeteria, the business office, a security guard at nineteen.

And always, out every window that faced it, the lake. You could take your lunch out to the patio and look at the water. You could have your tonsils out as a child and be too young to appreciate the lake view. On the Fourth of July you wanted to be on shift, because you could go up on the roof, and it was the best view ever for the fireworks.

Outside was the park. The hill you rode a sled down, on the east side, to Bixler Lake. Football on the grass next to the hospital. The tennis courts, and the trees with branches that hung to the ground so you could go under them and hide. You could walk the hospital halls wet from swimming, to get something out of the vending machines and use the restrooms. When a helicopter was needed, it did not land on the roof. It came down in the grassy field across from the channel, by the railroad tracks, and the fire department went down and set out cones and brought it in.

Parkview bought the hospital, and in 2004 it moved out to a new building. The old one on the lake came down, and the library went up where it stood. Not everyone was sorry to see it go. Some were glad. If you love to read, it even seems fitting. At least it's not a parking lot. You can be born in that spot and work there again, years later, in the library. You can take a grandchild to Bixler Lake Park and point at the library and tell her that the hospital where she was born once stood right there.

But if you had your babies there, or you were born there yourself, it is sad coming back home and finding it gone. Home is the word for it. The new library is very nice. It stands on the spot where you were born.
