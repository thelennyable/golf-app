# I'll Never Forget That Day
*The tornado of July 14, 1992*

This one belongs to everybody who was in Kendallville that summer, and to the ones who were away and found out from the national news. There is a question the town still asks itself, and every person over a certain age has an answer ready: where were you? At the fair. At work. On the porch. In the bathtub. In Walmart. Down in a basement with a flashlight. Driving home through rain you could not see through. Nearly every answer ends the same way. I'll never forget that day.

It was fair week, and it was Kids Day at the Noble County fair. The fairgrounds were packed with children riding the rides and parents and babysitters and grandmothers keeping count of them. Then the announcement came over the loudspeaker, and everybody was herded into the Merchants Building, or the beef and dairy arena, or any cement building close by. Some parents did not wait. They gathered the kids and ran for home, down Park Avenue, down to Wayne Street, to Diamond and Oak, and got to the basement just in time. You might have been one of the lucky ones who never made it to the fair at all, because your three-year-old had a meltdown and needed a nap, or because you looked at the sky and told the kids a storm was brewing and you would come back later.

If you were in the Merchants Building, you sat there and listened to the rain and hail cross the metal roof, and when the door opened you could see the rain driven sideways, nearly flat to the ground, and you thought: this is a tornado. And then it was over. If you were shut inside the little Pizza Hut stand with the ovens just turned off, you held the door cracked for air while the wind tried to pull it out of your hand, and what stayed with you was the crackling sound of the trees.

All over town, the signs were the same. The sky went from black to green. Low green clouds, a greenish purple, a strange yellow and pink that nobody had seen before or since. The rain went sideways past the living room window. A wall of water came across the parking lot. It sounded like a freight train. Plate glass bowed in and out of its frame, a picture window bent and did not break, and at the library the big windows shook in their rubber gaskets while the staff hurried everyone down to the basement to sit in the dark. You could smell it in the air, if you knew what you were smelling. A great many people did not. They thought it was just a bad rainstorm. They delivered the paper through it, drove to the post office in it, stood on the porch and watched it, and only found out afterward what had gone over their heads.

It formed on the west side and moved east. It took the bowling alley on Park Avenue. It narrowly missed the fairgrounds and all those children. It hit the shopping center out at Fairview, and the old Walmart, where the vestibule blew apart and the doors fell in and glass came through the store. If you were in there that day, you may still walk out of a store when it storms. Nobody quite agrees how much of that roof went: a corner of it rolling back, the whole thing spiraling up into the air, a unit off the top tossed into the little yogurt shop around the corner, water pouring into the store like a waterfall. It went through downtown, where you might have been in the basement of the beauty shop on Main Street with your hair pulled up in a plastic cap. It went down Mitchell Street, and at the factory there the ceiling moved like a wave as it passed. The way the town tells it, it hopped over Shepherd of the Hill and all the old folks inside, and came down again on Arvada Hills, where houses that were not a year old were simply gone.

People took shelter wherever they were. In the bathtub, with a blanket over the top, or with your mother pulling the shower curtain shut while you wondered what on earth that was going to do. In a closet with your brother and the dog. In a dark, damp basement with a whole day care of children who thought it was a great adventure. And some people did not take shelter at all. They stood on the front porch, with a grandmother or an infant daughter, and watched it come.

Then it was quiet, and the town walked out to see. You walked home down Dowling with the trees down on the cars and the power lines down in the street, and what you remember is the look of awe and confusion on every face you passed. You walked because there was no driving; the tops of the trees had been ripped off and were lying across the streets. Big old trees along Mitchell and Riley and Rush were down, and some people cried on the sidewalk over them, because a roof can be fixed and a hundred-year-old tree cannot. Your magnolia might have been twisted around itself clear to the ground. The power was out for days. There was no cable, so you missed the All-Star Game.

And then the town went to work. Street crews ran payloaders and dump trucks for weeks. Crews came from Auburn and Garrett and Avilla and Albion and Chain O'Lakes, and fire departments from all over the area. Firefighters worked a week straight. Somebody bought a chainsaw on the drive home. Neighbors came to help neighbors, and when you think of that day you think of the people who stepped up.

Nobody was killed. Say it to anybody in Kendallville and you will hear it back as something close to a prayer: God was watching over us that day. Kids Day at the fair, a store full of shoppers, a nursing home full of the elderly, and nobody killed.

Within a day there was a T-shirt, sold uptown, and a surprising number of them are still folded in closets and dresser drawers more than thirty years on. For a surprising number of people, it was also a birthday. A child turned ten, somebody's little brother, a woman turned thirty; you might have been cutting your birthday cake.

The bowling alley never came back. For years you could still see the lanes in the concrete where it stood, and now your own kids do not believe there was ever a bowling alley out by the fairgrounds. The old Walmart is a Rural King. The houses in Arvada Hills were rebuilt. But you can still drive down Mitchell Street and know exactly which trees are missing, because you were there the day they fell, and you'll never forget that day.
