# I Loved North Side
*North Side Elementary and the Kendallville grade schools*

This one belongs to the five-year-olds. Before anything else in Kendallville was yours, there was a grade school, and it had a name. North Side. South Side. Central. Wayne Center. Plenty of kids went to two or three of them before they were done, and a North Side kid will tell you all the cool kids went to North Side, and a Wayne Center kid will answer that they had cool kids at Wayne Center too.

Ask who went to North Side and the answers come back like roll call. I went to North Side. I went there. I loved Northside. Kindergarten through fifth, or kindergarten only, or just fifth grade because that was your first year in Kendallville. It does not seem to matter how long you were there. You loved North Side.

Kindergarten meant Mrs. Rasp, and for some North Side kids it meant her twice, because she turned up again in third grade. She was so kind. She was the favorite. Every student should have a Mrs. Rasp in their life. First grade was often Mrs. Woodworth. There was also a first-grade teacher remembered as the sweetest lady you could ever want to meet, a true sweetheart who genuinely loved her students. And North Side is where you might have met your very first friend, the one who befriended you as the new kid, and who may be your best friend still.

Music was Mrs. Irons. Cosette Irons, if you want the whole name, and a marvelous music teacher. There was a piano in every classroom. Her classes were always fun, and the old songs she taught are still in there. She could be the grown-up outside your family who gave you confidence and always pushed you to do more. She taught art too, which is easy to forget over the singing.

Then there was the library, and Mrs. Fulk. Dear Mrs. Fulk, the librarian, a favorite of everyone. The library was hands-up, seven-up at the library tables. It was where you went when the bookmobile came and you could buy books and other fun stuff. In fifth grade, the boys had their own filmstrip and the girls had theirs, *Your Changing Body*, and so much for all the little names the moms used instead of just saying it. What an embarrassing day. What an innocent age. And there was the little shop where you could buy folders, pencils, cap erasers.

Teachers read to you. There was the fourth-grade teacher who was always reading aloud, and the teacher who read the Little House books, life on the prairie, whose name is hard to bring back and then there it is. Over at Central, Mrs. Lennon read her fourth grade the original *Winnie the Pooh*, and everyone loved her. If you asked whether the library book you had picked was too babyish, she would tell you never to worry about that: if you enjoy it and you're reading, that's the important thing. In sixth grade there was a reading machine, where you had to keep trying to read faster and faster.

Recess at North Side was kickball, every day, in fifth grade. It was the merry-go-round, and yes, you could get sick on it. It was the hill on the playground in winter, and once they paved the hill, it was the hill you could break an arm sliding down. For a while there were tags: you wore one at recess, and if you didn't get your tag pulled, you got play cash to spend at the end of the year. Lots of play cash, for a slightly less than medium amount of stuff. Inside was the green brick gym, where you could bust out your two front teeth.

Not all of it was sweet. There was a paddle in the principal's office. You might never lay eyes on it, and the stories were scary enough. You might earn it by sticking your head out of the bus window one too many times.

And there was mischief. The fifth-grade room had a record player in one corner, and before class you could softly play records brought from home, until *Another Brick in the Wall* came on and the volume went way up for *hey, teacher, leave them kids alone*, and the whole class got shooed back to its seats. There were pencils stuck in the ceiling. There was the summer the building was being remodeled, when a bunch of girls snuck into the boys' restroom to see what a urinal looked like. If you were the new girl, you could be handed the word *Kendallville* in the third-grade spelling bee and get it wrong, and still love the teacher anyway.

Then there were the class pictures, all of you together on the stage, row after row, with the teacher. Look at one now and you can still name everyone but two. You were cute and innocent then.

The old North Side is gone, and there is a newer North Side now. If you toured the old building before they tore it down, you would have found many of the very same books in the library that you had read some thirty years before. They had waited. It was you that had gone on without them.
